## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Here's a great example.

At **Norfolk Southern**, Matt faced a significant challenge when a quality crisis emerged during the modernization of their Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. Despite having near-complete test coverage, defects were rising, and the client raised concerns. The situation was compounded by the shift to remote work due to the pandemic, which added complexity to addressing the issue.

Matt's leadership style, which emphasizes transparency and learning from failures, was put to the test. Instead of defending the metrics, he took ownership of the failure in front of both the client and **Accenture**'s account leadership. He discovered that an engineer had been writing tests after code development, which made the coverage look complete but allowed defects to slip through. The backlog itself was part of the problem, with vague stories lacking defined scenarios or a clear definition of done.

To address this, Matt implemented hands-on coaching using the Samman style, which included boot camps, coding katas, and practice learning sessions. He restructured agile ceremonies to ensure alignment among stakeholders, developers, and product managers. This approach helped the team rebuild discipline and improve story quality.

The result was a significant drop in defect rates as the quality of stories and test-first discipline improved. The client stopped finding defects that the dashboard couldn't see, restoring confidence in the team's work. This experience reinforced the importance of consistent coaching and cultural transformation beyond just process changes.

### B

🐾 Here's a story where Matt's leadership style faced challenges.

While leading a global platform modernization delivery for a **Fortune 500 financial services client**, Matt encountered a significant setback due to assumptions about existing systems. He assumed that the Identity and Access Management (IAM) controls, environment boundaries, and data masking were correctly implemented. This assumption led to an offshore tester accidentally triggering an email job in the User Acceptance Testing (UAT) environment, which sent communications containing unmasked production data to thousands of real clients.

The incident highlighted the risks of relying on inherited documentation and failing to verify critical security configurations. Matt took accountability for the oversight and led his team through a root cause analysis and remediation process. He learned that assumptions could disguise risks and that verifying IAM configurations, environment setups, and data handling protocols should be day-one responsibilities in his leadership approach.

The outcome was a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence, as the client demanded the removal of the tester from the project. This experience reinforced Matt's belief that leaders must own the environment their teams operate in and ensure that all assumptions are thoroughly validated to prevent similar issues in the future.
