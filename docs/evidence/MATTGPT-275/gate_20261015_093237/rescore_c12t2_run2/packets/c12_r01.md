## Turn 1. Visitor: Compare his Fiserv and JP Morgan ACCESS work.

### A

🐾 Here’s how the two engagements compare.

Both involved Execution & Delivery, but **Fiserv** also required Org & Working-Model Transformation: Matt took over a troubled program and changed how the team worked. At **JP Morgan Chase**, he led custom engineering and legacy consolidation for ACCESS Next Generation, with Risk & Responsible Tech work spanning payment security, entitlements, and regional compliance.

At **Fiserv**, the **\$8.5M** white-label card portal had missed multiple deadlines, quality was poor, and stakeholders were threatening contract cancellation. The **45-person** team worked through **200-page** requirements documents and **6-month** release cycles. Payment processing, fraud detection, and hosting vendors lacked coordinated dependencies. At **JP Morgan Chase**, the challenge was different: Bottomline’s WebSeries platform needed bespoke capabilities for corporate treasury operations, while fragmented regional systems and jurisdiction-specific rules complicated consolidation.

Matt’s coordination at **Fiserv** centered on recovery: daily offshore/onshore handoffs, coordinated deliverables across **3** vendors, persona-based user stories, and bi-weekly demos. He negotiated dedicated stakeholder review windows and retained steering committee reporting while adding sprint-level visibility. PMO and compliance accepted JIRA as the system of record; screen reader audits became part of Definition of Done. His ADA manifesto became the portal’s accessibility standard.

For ACCESS, Matt managed a **40+** blended team and spent **70–80%** of his time embedded at Bottomline’s Portsmouth offices. He worked with enterprise architects, security specialists, regional compliance officers, and developers to determine where WebSeries configuration ended and custom engineering began. His work covered ACH, wire, FX, MAC security, payment-specific RBAC, reporting, SSO, and a gateway across **12 countries**. He coordinated migration without disrupting live payments and sequenced regional launches by regulatory readiness rather than a global big-bang deployment.

**Fiserv** moved to **2-week** sprints within **3 months** and delivered **3% under budget**, meeting **47/47 acceptance criteria** with **zero critical defects** at launch. A recovered release avoided **\$500K** in penalties. ACCESS launched in **April 2011** as **JP Morgan Chase’s** unified global corporate banking portal; entitlement administration fell from days to minutes, and regional compliance configurations enabled launch without regulatory blockers.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Which was bigger?

### A

🐾 **JP Morgan Chase ACCESS had the broader platform scope; **Fiserv** had a documented budget and a similarly sized team.**

ACCESS served **135,000+** corporations, financial institutions, governments, and municipalities across **180+ countries**. Matt managed a **40+ person** blended team delivering payment modules, security, entitlements, reporting, and integrations. His payments gateway work covered **12 countries**, alongside legacy-system migration and regional compliance configuration.

The **Fiserv** portal served **2M+ cardholders** across **15 financial institutions**. Matt coordinated a **45-person** team and **3 external vendors**, managing an **\$8.5M**, **14-month** program. The portal processed **\$45M in transactions in its first month**.

So, by geographic reach and breadth of platform work, **ACCESS was bigger**. The documented team sizes were comparable; the customer counts measure different populations—corporate organizations versus individual cardholders. **Fiserv’s** budget was **\$8.5M**; an ACCESS budget isn’t stated, so there’s no supported budget comparison.
