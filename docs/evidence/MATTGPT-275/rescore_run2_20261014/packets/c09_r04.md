Matt co-created a developer portal with Java, .NET, and Python SDKs, use case-driven documentation, and a secure sandbox. He introduced environment virtualization for earlier testing, used Lean Product and XP practices, and conducted a Client Onboarding Test Drive. At **RBC**, Matt designed cross-border workflows from initiation through settlement and reconciliation. He defined real-time FX integration, automated alerts, recurring payments, Interac e-Transfer, and batch processing. The work produced an **implementation blueprint**, while revised cutoff processes reduced wire-transfer delays and improved SLA compliance.

## Turn 2. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership—and what he did when teams were struggling.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. Across his roles, he inherited engineering teams that were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” He made intent explicit, pushed decisions to the people closest to the work, and used transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. He coached teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks.

At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, teams had avoided risks because they feared blame. Matt introduced a “permit to fail” environment: non-punitive post-mortems, transparent sharing of mistakes, and low-stakes experiments with structured hypotheses. In product incubation, he set expectations that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail intentionally. Teams used those results to shape product direction week by week, reducing wasted engineering effort before committing to costly builds.

He also brought engineers, product managers, and UX architects into balanced teams with equal participation and shared accountability. Pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and knowledge-sharing sessions replaced siloed handoffs. Teams reported improved satisfaction and retention, reduced project delays, and achieved **zero-defect production code**.

At **Norfolk Southern**, that approach included taking ownership when things went wrong. The client reported rising defects despite near-complete test coverage. Matt acknowledged the failure in front of the client and account leadership rather than defending the dashboard. He audited the code and tests, exposed gaps in backlog stories, and brought TDD experts to work alongside engineers. Coaching followed an “I do, we do, you do” progression, with boot camps, coding katas, and practice learning sessions.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Across the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved **4x delivery acceleration** once they embraced lean product and modern engineering practices.

## Turn 3. Visitor: Give me an example.

### A

🐾 Here’s an example from **Norfolk Southern**.

In early **2020**, Matt’s first major Cloud Innovation Center engagement was modernizing the client’s legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months in, as the pandemic forced the team into remote work, the client reported rising defects—even though dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The concerns landed in front of both the client and **Accenture** account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt took ownership of the failure rather than defending the metrics. He ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly. An engineer had been building from his own interpretation instead of the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. The backlog also lacked defined scenarios and a definition of done, leaving no clear way to test the requirements.

Matt rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories. He brought frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work alongside the engineers and redo the code and tests properly. Coaching followed an “I do, we do, you do” progression, with boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and pairing.

After the experts left, he reinforced the daily workflow: test cases before the solution, rotating pairs, and checks at standup. New engineers paired with seasoned practitioners, and stories had to be small enough to finish within a **1-week** iteration.

Defect rates dropped, and the client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Client confidence returned through the open audit and visible improvement. The practices held after the experts went home, and the failure mode that caused the crisis did not recur.
