## Turn 1. Visitor: Tell me about his payments work.

### A

🐾 Here’s Matt’s payments work at **JP Morgan Chase** and **American Express**.

At **JP Morgan Chase**, Execution & Delivery centered on ACCESS Next Generation, the bank’s unified corporate banking portal. Its **135,000+** corporate, financial institution, government, and municipal clients across **180+** countries needed payment capabilities beyond Bottomline Technologies’ WebSeries offering. Regional systems had different data models, entitlement structures, and compliance records; payment types had distinct formatting, validation, and workflow requirements.

As Technology Delivery Lead, Matt managed a **40+** blended onshore/offshore team across Mumbai, Bangalore, and the US. He designed and delivered ACH, wire, FX/cross-border, and government tax payment modules, along with Message Authentication Code security and payment-type-specific permissions. He also delivered the centralized payments gateway connecting clearing networks and internal compliance, settlement, and reporting systems across **12** countries. Instead of separate regional integrations and logins, corporate treasurers received a single entry point for Wire, ACH, and Cross-Border Transfers. Matt led the payments data migration with **zero disruption** to live payment processing, preserving client configurations, transaction histories, and compliance traceability. ACCESS launched in **April 2011**.

At **American Express**, his work focused on adoption of the Virtual Payments Platform replacing a legacy mainframe system. Missing SDKs, fragmented documentation, dependency bottlenecks, and no sandbox were slowing client integration. Matt co-created the developer portal, developed Java, .NET, and Python SDKs, and introduced use case-driven documentation and secure, virtualized testing environments. He used Lean Product and XP practices and conducted a Client Onboarding Test Drive. API consumption and trial usage increased within **3 months** of relaunch; onboarding time fell, and the portal team exceeded its delivery commitment by over **261 story points** of customer-focused value.

## Turn 2. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes—and practices—his leadership.

Matt leads with “empathy, clarity, and purpose.” He describes teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, a rigid hierarchical culture also made people slow to share ideas. Other teams avoided risks because they feared blame, which slowed delivery and discouraged experimentation.

Matt made intent explicit and pushed decisions to the people closest to the work. He used transparency, direct feedback, and open communication, and coached teams “to think clearly, not just execute tasks.” He brought product managers, designers, and engineers together in balanced teams, delivering thin-slice, production-ready solutions so they could get user feedback and adjust.

His “permit to fail” environment had specific practices behind it: non-punitive post-mortems, low-stakes experiments, and structured hypotheses. Teams analyzed mistakes without pointing fingers and applied the lessons in subsequent sprints to prevent the same failure patterns. In early-stage product work, he set expectations that prototypes and user tests would likely fail intentionally—those results became evidence for deciding what to build next, rather than reasons to assign blame.

He also established weekly brainstorming sessions where ideas were considered without judgment. He de-emphasized individual accolades and recognized contributions that advanced shared project goals.

Across a **150-person** Cloud Innovation Center, his leadership story reports **4x delivery acceleration** once teams embraced lean product and modern engineering practices. The permit-to-fail work reduced repeat errors and shortened project timelines; the experimentation work reduced wasted engineering effort by testing assumptions before committing to costly builds.

## Turn 3. Visitor: Give me an example.

### A

🐾 Here’s an example from **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center.

Matt designed the center to be flat from day one: autonomy at the team level, collective ownership, and minimal middle layers. But many practitioners arrived from legacy delivery organizations where top-down, command-and-control patterns were the norm. Those habits reasserted themselves: decisions drifted upward, teams waited for direction, and bottlenecks formed where autonomy was supposed to be. Converting people to the model took enforcement and revalidation, multiple times; the path was bumpy.

Matt established cross-functional teams comprising product managers, designers, and engineers, with shared ownership and decision-making authority across disciplines. Leaders acted as facilitators rather than decision-makers. Anchors and team leads focused on removing obstacles rather than controlling outcomes. He created clear, flexible guidelines so teams could make decisions while staying within project objectives.

He also addressed what kept people from speaking up. As the centers scaled to **150+ practitioners**, pockets of fear of blame persisted. Matt regularly met with team members to understand their challenges and aspirations, conducted open retrospectives, and shared his own experiences with risk-taking and mistakes.

When something went wrong, he organized non-punitive post-mortems rather than pointing fingers. Teams identified lessons and applied them in subsequent sprints to prevent the same failure patterns. He supported low-stakes experiments using structured hypotheses and controlled iterations.

The reported results were fewer decision bottlenecks, higher team engagement and satisfaction, and improved retention. Teams began experimenting more freely; repeat errors declined, and project timelines shortened.
