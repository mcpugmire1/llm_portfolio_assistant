# Conversation 12, run 5

## Turn 1. Visitor: Compare his Fiserv and JP Morgan ACCESS work.

### A

🐾 Here’s how the two engagements compare.

Both involved Execution & Delivery, but the starting problems were different. At **Fiserv**, Matt inherited an **\$8.5M** white-label card portal roughly **7 months** into an engagement with missed deadlines, poor quality, and threatened contract cancellation. At **JP Morgan Chase**, ACCESS Next Generation required custom payment, security, and integration capabilities beyond Bottomline Technologies’ WebSeries platform, while consolidating fragmented regional systems under aggressive delivery timelines.

At **Fiserv**, Matt’s work centered on recovering delivery and changing how the **45-person** team worked. Developers in India, testers in the Philippines, and designers in Ireland lacked visibility into work status; requirements lived in **200-page** documents. He decomposed requirements into persona-based user stories, restructured JIRA, introduced bi-weekly demos, and moved the team from **6-month** release cycles to **2-week** sprints within **3 months**. PMO and compliance resisted agile; JIRA’s audit trails, attached accessibility scans, and developer comments satisfied their requirements. He also replaced SVN with Git, introduced mandatory reviews and automated quality gates, and coordinated **3 external vendors** through the mainframe-to-API transition.

At **JP Morgan Chase**, Matt managed a **40+** blended team and worked embedded at Bottomline’s Portsmouth offices. He designed and delivered ACH, wire, FX/cross-border, and tax-payment modules, payment verification, payment-specific permissions, and contextual help. Corporate administrators had been spending days configuring individual permissions; his grouping capability and role-based access controls reduced entitlement management from **days to minutes**. The payments gateway connected payment rails across **12 countries**, while SSO and global reporting reduced separate logins and fragmented treasury workflows. He also led payments-workstream migration without disruption to live payment operations.

The delivery proof differs. **Fiserv** finished **3% under budget**, met **47 acceptance criteria**, and launched with **zero critical defects**; Matt recovered a delayed release that avoided **\$500K** in penalties. ACCESS launched in **April 2011** as a unified corporate banking portal serving **135,**000+ client**s** across **180+ countries**. **Fiserv** was recovery of a troubled card-portal program; ACCESS was bespoke payments engineering and consolidation into a global treasury platform.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Which was bigger?

### A

🐾 ACCESS had the broader global scope; the team sizes were comparable.

**JP Morgan Chase’s** ACCESS platform served **135,000+ corporate, financial institution, government, and municipal clients across 180+ countries**. Matt managed a **40+ person** blended team delivering custom payment modules, security controls, and integrations. His payments gateway connected payment rails across **12 countries**.

**Fiserv’s** portal served **2M+ cardholders across 15 financial institutions**. Matt coordinated a **45-person team and 3 external vendors** on a **\$8.5M**, **14-month** program. The portal processed **\$45M in transactions in its first month**.

So ACCESS was broader geographically and covered more treasury functions. **Fiserv** had the larger stated end-user count—but cardholders and institutional clients are different measures. There’s no ACCESS budget figure in these stories, so a dollar-for-dollar comparison isn’t available.
