Matt brought business and technical stakeholders together through North Star and Discovery and Framing workshops. He co-created a developer portal, developed Java, .NET, and Python SDKs, introduced use-case-driven documentation and virtualized testing environments, and conducted a Client Onboarding Test Drive.

API consumption and trial usage increased within **3 months** of relaunch, onboarding time decreased, and the portal team delivered **over 261 additional story points** beyond its commitment.

## Turn 2. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose—pushing decisions to the people closest to the work and coaching teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks.

At **Accenture**’s Cloud Innovation Center, many practitioners came from command-and-control delivery organizations. Although the center was designed flat from day one, old habits returned: decisions drifted upward, teams waited for direction, and bottlenecks formed. Early on, a rigid hierarchical culture also made people slow to share ideas. Moving to collective ownership required repeated enforcement and revalidation; the path was bumpy.

Matt established balanced teams of product managers, designers, and engineers with shared ownership and decision-making authority. Anchors and team leads focused on removing obstacles rather than controlling outcomes. He made intent explicit, used direct feedback and open communication, and established weekly brainstorming sessions where ideas were considered without judgment. He recognized contributions that advanced shared project goals rather than emphasizing individual accolades.

That coaching also happened when delivery went wrong. At **Norfolk Southern**, defects were rising despite near-complete test coverage. Matt took ownership in front of the client and **Accenture** account leadership rather than defending the dashboard. He audited the code and tests, exposed gaps in both the implementation and backlog, and brought in TDD experts to work side by side with engineers. Coaching followed an “I do, we do, you do” progression, supported by coding katas and practice learning sessions. The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see.

Across the center, Matt paired senior engineers with junior colleagues, organized peer-led demos and engineering practice forums, and embedded learning in live client projects. His leadership-philosophy story reports **4x delivery acceleration** once teams embraced lean product and modern engineering practices.

## Turn 3. Visitor: Give me an example.

### A

🐾 Here’s an example from **Norfolk Southern**.

In early **2020**, Matt’s Cloud Innovation Center team was modernizing the railroad’s legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months into the engagement, as the pandemic shifted work remote, the client reported rising defects—even though the team’s dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The concerns landed in front of both the client and **Accenture** account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt took ownership of the failure rather than defending the metrics. He ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly. An engineer had been building from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done.

Instead of issuing a process reminder, Matt rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought in frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work side by side with engineers. They redid the code and test cases, then coached through boot camps, coding katas, and practice learning sessions using an “I do, we do, you do” progression.

Afterward, he made test-first work visible in the team’s daily routine: test cases came before the solution, pairs rotated daily, and new engineers paired with seasoned practitioners. Remote pairs traded drivers frequently. Stories entered sprints only when testable and small enough for a **one-week** iteration.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Defect rates dropped, and the practices held after the experts left. The failure mode that caused the crisis did not recur.
