# Conversation 1, run 4

## Turn 1. Visitor: Tell me about his payments work.

### A

🐾 Here’s the story behind Matt’s payments work.

At **JP Morgan Chase**, Matt was Technology Delivery Lead for the payments workstream of ACCESS Next Generation. The bank’s regional and legacy payment systems had different data models, entitlement structures, message formats, and compliance requirements. Bottomline Technologies’ WebSeries platform supplied the foundation, but the bank needed purpose-built capabilities for **135,000+** clients across **180+** countries.

Matt managed a blended team of **40+** **Accenture** and **JP Morgan Chase** resources across Mumbai, Bangalore, and the US. He defined the onshore-offshore engagement model and spent **70–80%** of his time embedded at Bottomline’s Portsmouth offices, coordinating with bank stakeholders in Chicago and NY/NJ. He managed the build-vs-configure boundary: deciding where WebSeries’ native capabilities ended and bank-specific engineering was required.

He designed and delivered ACH, wire, FX/cross-border, and federal tax payment modules, with payment-type-specific entitlements, Message Authentication Code security, compliance validation, and contextual guidance. He also designed the centralized gateway for message transformation, protocol translation, and routing across **12 countries**, connecting ACCESS to clearing networks and internal compliance, settlement, and reporting systems.

Alongside the build, Matt coordinated with compliance teams on legacy-data verification and migration. Client configurations, entitlements, and transaction histories moved into ACCESS with **zero disruption to live payment processing**. He owned production incident response, bringing bank, offshore, and vendor teams together for root-cause analysis, status updates, and daily leadership calls.

ACCESS launched in **April 2011**, consolidating regional systems into a unified corporate banking portal. At **RBC**, Matt subsequently established the PMO during discovery and requirements for Day**2Day** Corporate Banking, while the bank evaluated Bottomline as its payment hub engine and planned to sunset **RBC** Express. He brought **RBC**, **Accenture**, and Bottomline together through governance meetings, designated contacts, shared updates, and task tracking.

## Turn 3. Visitor: What went wrong?

### A

🐾 There were production defects—not just delivery complexity.

At **JP Morgan Chase**, a Message Authentication Code (MAC) layer failure affected the security mechanism used to verify payment integrity before release. Matt’s root-cause investigation traced it to **missing unit tests and weak error handling**. He had break-glass production access and queried live payment data via SQL to triage the platform’s hardest defects. The story does not specify the failure’s duration, affected transaction count, or financial impact.

The migration also carried problems forward from the legacy systems. After cutover, production defects emerged that were primarily **data issues carried over from the legacy platform during migration**. Regional systems had different schemas, client configurations, entitlement structures, and compliance records; consolidating them did not eliminate every underlying data issue. Matt ran UAT and production validation/shakeout testing and triaged the defects during rollout. The migration nevertheless completed without disruption to live payment operations.

Across successive gateway releases, waves, and migrations, Matt repeatedly owned Sev-**1** incident response for a live global payments platform processing real customer transactions. He coordinated root-cause analysis across the bank’s New York team, engineers in Mumbai and Bangalore, and Bottomline’s vendor team. While incidents were live, he ran status updates and daily leadership calls.

There were also pre-existing operational problems: entitlement changes took days of manual configuration, with poor visibility into who could access which payment functions. Matt replaced individual permission management with role-based profiles, bulk grouping, and payment-type-specific controls, reducing entitlement management from days to minutes.

The concrete failures described are the MAC-layer defect and post-cutover legacy-data defects—not a failed overall launch. ACCESS launched in **April 2011**.
