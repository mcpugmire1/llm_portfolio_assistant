# Conversation 12, run 3

## Turn 1. Visitor: Compare his Fiserv and JP Morgan ACCESS work.

### A

🐾 Here’s how the two engagements compare.

Both involved Execution & Delivery; **Fiserv** also required changing how the team worked. Its **\$8.5M** white-label card portal had missed consecutive deadlines, quality was poor, and stakeholders were threatening contract cancellation. At **JP Morgan Chase**, ACCESS Next Generation needed custom payment, security, and entitlement capabilities beyond Bottomline’s WebSeries platform, under aggressive Wall Street delivery timelines.

At **Fiserv**, Matt took over as Development Manager roughly **7 months** into the engagement. He coordinated a **45-person** team and **3 external vendors** covering payment processing, fraud detection, and hosting. Instead of continuing with **6-month** release cycles and **200-page** requirements documents, he introduced **2-week** sprints, persona-based user stories, and bi-weekly demos. He ran **7 AM** and **11 PM** handoff calls, negotiated dedicated stakeholder review windows, and addressed PMO and compliance resistance by establishing JIRA as the system of record, with audit trails and attached accessibility scans. He also wrote the ADA manifesto that became the portal’s accessibility standard.

For **JP Morgan Chase** ACCESS, Matt was Technology Delivery Lead for a **40+** blended team across the US, Mumbai, and Bangalore. He spent **70–80%** of his time embedded at Bottomline’s Portsmouth offices, managing the boundary between native platform capabilities and bespoke engineering. He worked with enterprise architects, security SMEs, bank stakeholders, and developers on payment-type-specific permissions and AML/KYC requirements. He delivered ACH, wire, FX/cross-border modules, MAC security, and the centralized payments gateway, while owning production incident response and daily leadership communications.

The **Fiserv** program finished **3% under budget**, met **47/47 acceptance criteria**, and launched with **zero critical defects**. Recovering a delayed holiday release avoided **\$500K in penalties**; the engagement secured a **\$3M contract extension**.

ACCESS launched in **April 2011**, serving **135,**000+ clients** across 180+ countries**. Its gateway supported payments across **12 countries**, while the redesigned entitlement engine reduced administration from days to minutes through bulk permission changes and centralized visibility.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Which was bigger?

### A

🐾 **JP Morgan Chase ACCESS was bigger in global reach and platform scope; **Fiserv** had the larger stated delivery team.**

ACCESS served **135,000+ corporate, financial institution, government, and municipal clients across 180+ countries**. Matt led a **40+** blended team delivering payment modules, security, and a gateway supporting payments across **12 countries**, alongside the consolidation of regional systems into a unified portal.

**Fiserv** was an **\$8.5M**, **14-month** program with a **45-person** team and **3 external vendors**. Its white-label card portal served **2M+ cardholders across 15 financial institutions**.

Those customer counts measure different things—individual cardholders versus institutional clients—so they aren’t a like-for-like comparison. **Fiserv** has the documented budget; there isn’t an ACCESS budget figure in these stories to compare.
