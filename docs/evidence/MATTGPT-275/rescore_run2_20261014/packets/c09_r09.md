## Turn 2. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership—and what he did in practice.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. In teams he inherited, engineers were often “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” He made intent explicit, pushed decisions to the people closest to the work, and used transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, he introduced weekly brainstorming sessions without judgment and recognized contributions that advanced shared project goals rather than individual accolades.

His “permit to fail” environment included non-punitive post-mortems and lean, low-stakes experiments. Teams shared errors openly, applied lessons to subsequent sprints, and reduced recurring failure patterns. Coaching happened through senior–junior pairings, weekly tech talks, peer-led demos, and learning embedded in live client projects—not just separate training.

At **Norfolk Southern**, that approach was tested when defects rose despite near-complete test coverage. Matt took ownership in front of the client and **Accenture** account leadership instead of defending the dashboard. A bottom-up audit found tests written after the code and backlog stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done.

He rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought frontend and backend TDD experts to work side by side with engineers. The team practiced through boot camps, coding katas, and an “I do, we do, you do” progression. The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see, and defect rates dropped as test-first discipline took hold.

Across the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved **4x delivery acceleration** after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices.

## Turn 3. Visitor: Give me an example.

### A

🐾 Here’s an example from **Norfolk Southern**.

In early **2020**, Matt’s Cloud Innovation Center team was modernizing the legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months into the engagement, as the pandemic pushed everyone into remote work, the client reported rising defects—even though dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The concerns landed in front of both the client and **Accenture** account leadership.

Matt took ownership of the failure rather than defending the metrics. He ran a complete bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly. An engineer had been building from his own interpretation instead of the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. The backlog was also part of the problem: stories lacked defined scenarios and a definition of done.

Matt rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories. He brought frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work side by side with engineers and redo the code and test cases. Coaching included boot camps, coding katas, lunch and learns, and an “I do, we do, you do” progression.

After the experts left, he reinforced the practices in the team’s daily workflow. Pairs rotated daily, newer engineers worked with seasoned practitioners, and test cases came before the solution—a step checked at standup. Stories entered sprints only when testable and small enough for a **one-week** iteration. Remote pairs traded drivers frequently.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Defect rates dropped, client confidence returned, and **the failure mode did not recur**. Pairing and test-first remained the team’s default after the outside experts went home.
