Payment types had different formatting, validation, compliance, and workflow requirements. Regional systems also had different data models, entitlement structures, and transaction histories. At **American Express**, his work focused on adoption of the Virtual Payments Platform. Missing SDKs, fragmented documentation, no sandbox, and testing dependencies were slowing client integration. Matt brought business and technical stakeholders together through North Star and Discovery and Framing workshops. He co-created the developer portal, developed Java, .NET, and Python SDKs, added use case-driven documentation, and coordinated virtualized testing environments. A Client Onboarding Test Drive gathered end-user feedback.

API consumption and trial usage increased within **3 months** of relaunch. Onboarding time fell, and the portal team exceeded its delivery commitment by over **261 story points** of customer-focused value.

## Turn 2. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes—and practices—his leadership.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. In teams he inherited, engineers were often “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, a rigid hierarchical culture also made people slow to share ideas. Teams avoided risks because they feared blame, contributing to slower delivery and missed opportunities.

Matt made intent explicit and pushed decisions to the people closest to the work. He used transparency, direct feedback, and open communication, and coached teams to think clearly rather than just execute tasks. He brought product managers, designers, and engineers together in balanced teams, prioritizing essential features and delivering thin-slice, production-ready solutions for rapid user feedback.

His “permit to fail” environment had specific practices behind it: non-punitive post-mortems, low-stakes experiments, and weekly brainstorming sessions where ideas were considered without judgment. He emphasized shared goals over individual accolades and recognized contributions that advanced the team’s mission. Post-mortem lessons were applied in subsequent sprints to prevent identified failure patterns from recurring.

In early-stage product work, Matt set expectations that prototypes and user tests would likely fail—and that this was intentional. Teams used user research, JTBD framing, and rapid testing to validate assumptions before committing to costly builds. Failed experiments became decision inputs rather than setbacks.

He paired that environment with automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first engineering practices. At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved **4x delivery acceleration** once they embraced lean product and modern engineering practices. The change did not happen overnight; teams also reduced repeat errors and shortened delivery timelines.

## Turn 3. Visitor: Give me an example.

### A

🐾 Here’s an example from **Norfolk Southern**.

In early **2020**, Matt’s Cloud Innovation Center team was modernizing the client’s legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months into the engagement, as the pandemic moved work fully remote, the client reported rising defects—even though the team’s dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The concerns landed in front of both the client and **Accenture** account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt took ownership of the failure rather than defending the metrics. He ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and shared the gaps openly. An engineer had been working from his own interpretation instead of the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. The backlog was also part of the problem: stories lacked defined scenarios and a definition of done, leaving no clear way to test them.

Matt rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories. He brought frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work side by side with the team, then had the code and test cases redone properly. Rather than relying on a process change alone, he used the Samman coaching approach: boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, lunch and learns, and pairing engineers with subject-matter experts through an “I do, we do, you do” progression. He also restructured agile ceremonies and introduced product management and SMART story training.

Defect rates dropped as story quality and test-first discipline took hold. The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see, and confidence was restored through the open audit and visible improvement.
