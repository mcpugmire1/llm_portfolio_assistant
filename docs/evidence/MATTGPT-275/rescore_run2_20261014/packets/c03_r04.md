# Conversation 3, run 4

## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes—and practices—his leadership style.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” His focus was to remove those barriers: make intent explicit, build psychological safety so people could speak honestly, and push decisions to the people closest to the work.

At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, that meant a “permit to fail” environment rather than fear of blame. Matt organized non-punitive post-mortems and supported low-stakes experiments with structured hypotheses and controlled iterations. Teams applied the lessons in subsequent sprints, reduced repeat errors, and shortened project timelines. In product incubation, he set expectations that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail—and treated those results as evidence for deciding what to build next.

He also brought engineers, product managers, and UX architects into balanced teams with shared accountability. Pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and knowledge-sharing sessions replaced role silos and repeated handoffs. Teams could voice feedback and change their workflows autonomously; team satisfaction and retention improved.

At **Norfolk Southern**, that leadership included taking ownership when defects rose despite near-complete test coverage. Matt acknowledged the failure in front of the client and **Accenture** account leadership rather than defending the dashboard. He audited the code and tests, exposed vague backlog stories, and brought in TDD experts to work alongside engineers. Coaching followed an “I do, we do, you do” progression through coding katas, practice sessions, and pairing.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Across the Cloud Innovation Center, teams accelerated delivery **4x** once they embraced lean product and modern engineering practices.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 The initial rollout at **Norfolk Southern** didn’t work as intended.

In early **2020**, Matt launched the Cloud Innovation Center’s first major engagement: modernizing **Norfolk Southern’s** legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. He brought in a balanced team and emphasized test-driven development. But a couple of months in, as the pandemic forced remote work, the client reported rising defects—even though the dashboards showed near-complete test coverage.

Pairing, test-first development, and continuous integration had been in place from the start. What had been missing was reinforcement, follow-up, and verification. Developers had resisted the new ways of working, and the client and **Accenture** account leadership were accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt’s investigation found an engineer building from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete while defects went undetected. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done.

Matt took ownership of the failure in front of the client and account leadership. He audited the codebase and tests, exposed the gaps openly, rebuilt the backlog, and brought in frontend and backend TDD experts to redo the work alongside the team.

He then changed the daily workflow: test cases before the solution, inside rotating pairs, checked at standup. New engineers paired with seasoned practitioners, and stories entered sprints only when discrete and testable.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. The practices held after the experts left, and the crisis failure mode did not recur. Matt’s stated lesson was that “implementing a practice is the easy half”—reinforcement, follow-up, and verification were what had been missing.
