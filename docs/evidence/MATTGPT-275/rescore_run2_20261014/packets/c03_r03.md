# Conversation 3, run 3

## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes—and practices—his leadership style.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. Most engineering teams he inherited were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, practitioners often brought command-and-control habits from legacy delivery organizations: decisions drifted upward, teams waited for direction, and bottlenecks formed where autonomy was supposed to be. Changing those habits required enforcement and revalidation, multiple times; the path was bumpy.

Matt pushed decisions to the people closest to the work. He assembled balanced teams of product managers, designers, and engineers with shared ownership and decision-making authority. Leaders acted as facilitators rather than decision-makers, and team leads focused on removing obstacles rather than controlling outcomes. He made intent explicit, used transparency and direct feedback, and coached teams to think clearly—not just execute tasks.

His “permit to fail” environment included non-punitive post-mortems, transparent sharing of errors, and lean, low-stakes experiments. In innovation work, he set expectations that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail intentionally. Teams used user research, JTBD framing, and rapid testing to validate assumptions before committing to costly builds, then shaped product direction week by week.

That autonomy came with engineering practices: pair programming, collaborative coding, automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first practices. Retrospectives gave teams opportunities to change how they worked without waiting for permission.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved **4x delivery acceleration** after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. His balanced-team work achieved **zero-defect production code** through iterative development and real-time feedback loops. Experiments also reduced wasted engineering effort by eliminating wrong directions before engineering investment.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Yes—the first Cloud Innovation Center engagement with **Norfolk Southern** went wrong despite the balanced-team model and emphasis on test-driven development.

In early **2020**, Matt’s team began modernizing the client’s legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months in, as the pandemic forced work remote, the client reported rising defects. The dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The feedback caught the team off guard, and the credibility hit landed in front of both the client and **Accenture** account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt’s investigation found that an engineer had been solutioning from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete, but defects went undetected. The backlog was also part of the problem: vague stories lacked defined scenarios and a definition of done.

Matt took ownership of the failure rather than defending the metrics. He ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly. He rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought in frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work side by side with the team.

Instead of just restating the process, he used Samman-style coaching: boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and pairing engineers with subject-matter experts through an “I do, we do, you do” progression. The team redid the code and test cases and restructured agile ceremonies.

Defect rates dropped, and the client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. The story documents an initial failure followed by a course correction—not an engagement that remained unsuccessful.
