## Turn 1. Visitor: Tell me about his payments work.

### A

🐾 Here’s Matt’s payments work across **JP Morgan Chase**, **American Express**, and **RBC**.

At **JP Morgan Chase**, Matt was Technology Delivery Lead for the payment engine behind ACCESS Next Generation. Bottomline Technologies’ WebSeries platform supplied the foundation, but the bank needed payment modules, security controls, and workflows well beyond its out-of-box offering. Its regional legacy systems also had different data models, client configurations, and compliance records—all while live payments had to continue.

Matt managed a blended onshore/offshore team of **40+** and spent **70–80%** of his time embedded at Bottomline’s Portsmouth offices. He designed and delivered ACH, wire, FX/cross-border, and government tax payment modules. The custom engineering included embedded FX rate visibility, Message Authentication Code payment verification, payment-type-specific permissions, sanctions screening, and risk-based approval routing. He also delivered real-time global reporting and Single Sign-On across treasury functions.

He led the payments data migration, preserving client configurations, transaction histories, and compliance traceability **without disruption to live payment operations**. ACCESS launched in **April 2011**, serving **135,000+** clients across **180+** countries.

At **American Express**, the challenge was adoption of its Virtual Payments Platform: missing SDKs, fragmented documentation, no sandbox, and delayed testing. Matt co-created a developer portal with Java, .NET, and Python SDKs, real-world API documentation, and a secure sandbox with refreshed client data. Environment virtualization reduced downstream dependencies; a Client Onboarding Test Drive gathered end-user feedback. API trial usage increased within **3 months** of relaunch, onboarding time fell, and the portal team exceeded its delivery commitment by **261 story points**.

At **RBC**, Matt designed cross-border payment workflows from initiation through settlement and reconciliation, including real-time FX, automated alerts, recurring payments, Interac e-Transfer, and batch processing. He delivered an implementation blueprint ready for development; revised payment cutoff processes reduced wire-transfer delays and improved SLA compliance.

## Turn 3. Visitor: What went wrong?

### A

🐾 On **JP Morgan Chase’s** ACCESS payments work, there were production defects—not just delivery complexity.

The clearest incident was a failure in the Message Authentication Code (MAC) security layer, which let corporate users verify payment integrity before release. Matt used break-glass production access and queried live payment data via SQL to triage it. The root cause was **missing unit tests and weak error handling**. The account doesn’t specify the incident’s duration, affected payment volume, or financial impact.

There were also **post-cutover data defects**, primarily issues carried over from the legacy platform during migration. Regional systems had different schemas, formats, client configurations, entitlement structures, and compliance records. Matt ran UAT and production validation/shakeout testing and triaged defects that emerged after cutover. The migration account reports **no disruption to live payment operations**; that does not mean the rollout was defect-free.

Across successive releases, waves, and migrations, Matt repeatedly owned Sev-**1** incident response and on-call accountability. He coordinated root-cause analysis across New York, Mumbai, Bangalore, and Bottomline’s vendor team, while running status updates and daily leadership calls during live incidents.

Other problems predated ACCESS: fragmented payment connectivity across **12 countries**, entitlement changes taking days of manual configuration, and regional regulatory requirements that prevented a single big-bang deployment. Matt delivered a centralized gateway, bulk permission changes with payment-type-specific access controls, and regional launches sequenced by regulatory readiness.

ACCESS launched in **April 2011**. The documented failures were the MAC-layer defect and migrated legacy-data issues—not a failed platform launch.
