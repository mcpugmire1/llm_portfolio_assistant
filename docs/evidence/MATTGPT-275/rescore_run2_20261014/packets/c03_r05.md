# Conversation 3, run 5

## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes—and practices—his leadership.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” He makes intent explicit, pushes decisions to the people closest to the work, and uses transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. He coaches teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks.

At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, a rigid hierarchical culture initially made people slow to share ideas. Matt established weekly brainstorming sessions where ideas were considered without judgment. He emphasized the shared mission over individual accolades and recognized contributions that advanced project goals. He also paired senior engineers with junior team members, built peer learning communities, and embedded learning in live client projects.

His “permit to fail” environment includes setting expectations that early prototypes and experiments will likely fail. Teams use user research and rapid testing to challenge assumptions before committing to costly builds, then use the findings to shape product direction week by week.

At **Norfolk Southern**, rising defects contradicted near-complete test coverage. Matt took ownership in front of the client and **Accenture** account leadership rather than defending the dashboard. He openly audited the code and tests, rebuilt vague backlog items into SMART, testable stories, and brought frontend and backend TDD experts alongside the team. Coaching followed an “I do, we do, you do” progression. He also restructured agile ceremonies to keep stakeholders, developers, and product managers aligned.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Across the Cloud Innovation Center, teams accelerated delivery **4x** after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Yes—Matt describes a platform delivery where he relied on assumptions instead of verifying the environment his team was working in.

He was the tech lead on a global modernization engagement for a financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams running test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production. IAM controls, environment boundaries, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from earlier phases, along with documentation.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. Instead, he assumed the controls were correct and focused on delivery pressures. He did not audit who could trigger downstream email jobs, confirm that production customer data had been masked, or implement compensating controls such as email interception.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to **thousands** of real clients.

Matt took accountability and worked collectively with the team on root cause analysis and corrective controls. But that did not prevent an individual consequence: the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt describes it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

His stated change afterward was specific: IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership became day-one leadership responsibilities in his delivery approach. His wording for the failure is “assumptions are risks in disguise,” and his conclusion is that leaders own the environment their teams operate in.
