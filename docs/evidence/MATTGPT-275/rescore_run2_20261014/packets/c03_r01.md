# Conversation 3, run 1

## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt leads.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” His response is to make intent explicit, push decisions to the people closest to the work, and coach teams to think clearly—not just execute tasks.

At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, he established a “permit to fail” environment for teams that avoided risks because they feared blame. He organized non-punitive post-mortems, emphasized lessons rather than pointing fingers, and supported low-stakes experiments with structured hypotheses and controlled iterations. Teams applied those lessons in subsequent sprints, preventing recurrence of identified failure patterns and shortening project timelines.

He also assembled balanced teams of engineers, product managers, and UX architects with equal participation and shared accountability. Pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and open feedback replaced siloed handoffs. Those teams reduced project delays and achieved **zero-defect production code**. His leadership philosophy story reports **4x delivery acceleration** once teams embraced lean product and modern engineering practices.

At **Norfolk Southern**, that style included taking ownership when delivery went wrong. Defects were rising despite near-complete test coverage. Matt acknowledged the failure in front of the client and **Accenture** account leadership rather than defending the dashboard. He audited the code and tests, exposed gaps in the backlog, and rebuilt stories with defined scenarios and a definition of done.

He then brought in TDD experts to work alongside the team and used boot camps, coding katas, and an “I do, we do, you do” coaching progression. Defect rates dropped, and the client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Yes—the initial rollout at **Norfolk Southern** didn’t hold.

Matt launched the Cloud Innovation Center’s first major engagement in early **2020** with a balanced team, pair programming, and test-driven development. A couple of months in, as the pandemic moved the team to remote work, the client reported rising defects despite dashboards showing near-complete test coverage. The credibility hit landed in front of both the client and **Accenture** account leadership.

The practices were in place, but the follow-up discipline was missing. An engineer was solutioning from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story and writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete while defects went undetected. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done.

Matt took ownership of the failure rather than defending the metrics. He audited the codebase and test suite, showed the gaps openly, rebuilt the backlog, and brought in TDD experts to work alongside the engineers and redo the code and tests.

He then changed the daily workflow—not just the training. Test cases became a discrete step before engineering the solution, checked at standup. Pairs rotated daily, and newer engineers worked with seasoned practitioners. Stories entered sprints only when testable and small enough for a **one-week** iteration. Remote pairs traded drivers frequently to reduce fatigue and break mental blocks.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see, and the failure mode did not recur after the experts left. Matt’s stated lesson was that “implementing a practice is the easy half”—reinforcement, follow-up, and verification had been missing the first time.
