## Turn 2. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes—and practices—his leadership.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. Many engineering teams he inherited were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, the organization was designed flat from day one, but practitioners arriving from legacy delivery organizations sometimes returned to command-and-control habits. Decisions drifted upward, teams waited for direction, and bottlenecks formed. Changing those habits required repeated enforcement and revalidation.

Matt pushed decisions to the people closest to the work. He established cross-functional teams of product managers, designers, and engineers with shared ownership and decision-making authority. Anchors and team leads focused on removing obstacles rather than controlling outcomes. He made intent explicit, used direct feedback and open communication, and coached teams to think clearly—not just execute tasks.

When people were reluctant to share ideas, he introduced weekly brainstorming sessions where ideas were considered without judgment. He emphasized shared goals over individual accolades and recognized contributions that advanced the mission. For engineers facing cloud-native knowledge gaps, he paired senior engineers with junior team members, introduced peer-led demos and engineering practice forums, and embedded learning in live client projects. New hires onboarded faster, engineering proficiency improved, and retention increased.

In innovation work, Matt set expectations that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail—and that this was intentional. Teams used those results to change product direction before committing to costly builds, reducing wasted engineering effort.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved **4x delivery acceleration** after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. Teams also reported higher engagement and satisfaction as decentralized decisions removed bottlenecks.

## Turn 3. Visitor: Give me an example.

### A

🐾 Here’s an example from **Norfolk Southern**.

In early **2020**, Matt’s first major Cloud Innovation Center engagement was modernizing **Norfolk Southern’s** legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months in, as the pandemic moved everyone to remote work, the client reported rising defects—even though the team’s dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The concerns landed in front of both the client and **Accenture** account leadership.

Matt took ownership of the failure rather than defending the metrics. He dug into the code and discovered that an engineer had been building from his own interpretation instead of the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete, but defects went undetected. The backlog was also part of the problem: stories lacked defined scenarios and a definition of done.

He ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly. He rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work side by side with the team, redoing the code and test cases properly.

For the engineers, the correction included hands-on coaching—not just new process requirements. Matt introduced boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and pairing with subject-matter experts through an “I do, we do, you do” progression. Product managers received SMART story training, and agile ceremonies were restructured to keep stakeholders, developers, and product managers working from the same requirements.

Defect rates dropped as story quality and test-first discipline took hold. The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see, and confidence was restored through the open audit and visible improvement.
