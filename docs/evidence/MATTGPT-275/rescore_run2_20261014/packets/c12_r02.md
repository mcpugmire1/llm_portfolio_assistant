# Conversation 12, run 2

## Turn 1. Visitor: Compare his Fiserv and JP Morgan ACCESS work.

### A

🐾 Here’s how the two engagements compare.

At **Fiserv**, Matt took over a delivery recovery: an **\$8.5M** white-label card portal serving **2M+ cardholders** across **15 financial institutions**. The **45-person** team had missed multiple deadlines, production quality was poor, and stakeholders were threatening contract cancellation. At **JP Morgan Chase**, ACCESS required bespoke engineering beyond Bottomline Technologies’ WebSeries platform: payment modules, security controls, entitlements, and global connectivity for **135,**000+ client**s** across **180+ countries**.

At **Fiserv**, Matt served as Development Manager and also covered program management during the program lead’s paternity leave. He coordinated **3 external vendors** and managed the integration layer wrapping VisionPLUS **350** mainframe COBOL into a Java EE/Spring portal. He replaced **6-month** waterfall release cycles with **2-week** sprints, decomposed a **200-page** BRD into persona-based user stories, and introduced sprint demos to catch branding and ADA issues early. For builds and deployments, he replaced SVN with Git, mandated senior-developer reviews and unit tests, and introduced Maven, Artifactory, Hudson, and SonarQube quality gates.

For **JP Morgan Chase** ACCESS, Matt was Technology Delivery Lead for a **40+ person** blended team. He spent **70–80%** of his time embedded at Bottomline’s Portsmouth offices, managing the build-versus-configure boundary. He designed and delivered ACH, wire, FX/cross-border, and tax-payment capabilities; MAC payment verification; payment-type-specific RBAC; and SSO. The gateway connected payment channels across **12 countries**. Bulk entitlement changes replaced individual configuration, reducing administration from days to minutes. He also led payments data migration, preserving compliance traceability without disrupting live payment operations, and owned production incident response.

The **Fiserv** program delivered **3% under budget**, met **47/47 acceptance criteria**, and launched with **zero critical defects**. A recovered release avoided **\$500K in penalties**; the portal processed **\$45M** in its first month with **99.**97%** uptime**. ACCESS launched in **April 2011**, consolidating regional systems into a unified global corporate banking portal with purpose-built payments, centralized entitlements, and real-time global reporting.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Which was bigger?

### A

🐾 **JP Morgan Chase ACCESS was bigger in geographic reach and scope of the platform.** Team sizes were comparable; budget size can’t be compared from the available figures.

ACCESS served **135,000+ corporations, financial institutions, governments, and municipalities across 180+ countries**. Matt’s work covered bespoke payment modules, a gateway connecting payment channels across **12 countries**, security and entitlements, regional-system migration, and jurisdiction-specific compliance across APAC, EMEA, and the Americas. He managed a **40+ person** blended team.

**Fiserv** had the larger stated end-user count: **2M+ cardholders across 15 financial institutions**. Matt coordinated a **45-person** team and **3 external vendors** on a **14-month, **\$8.5**M** white-label card portal program.

Those audience counts aren’t directly equivalent: **Fiserv** counted individual cardholders; ACCESS counted organizations. And while **Fiserv’s **\$8.5**M** budget is documented, an ACCESS budget isn’t provided.
