## Turn 1. Visitor: Tell me about his payments work.

### A

🐾 Here’s Matt’s payments work.

At **JP Morgan Chase**, Matt was Technology Delivery Lead for ACCESS Next Generation, a corporate banking portal built on Bottomline Technologies’ WebSeries platform. The bank’s regional systems had different data models, payment formats, entitlement structures, and compliance records. The out-of-box platform did not cover the requirements of **135,000+** clients across **180+** countries.

Matt managed a blended team of **40+** across **Accenture** and the bank, with engineers in Mumbai, Bangalore, and the US. Rather than treating ACCESS as a standard platform deployment, he managed the build-versus-configure boundary: determining where WebSeries’s native capabilities ended and bespoke engineering was required. He designed and delivered ACH, wire, FX/cross-border, and tax-payment modules, including NACHA validation, payment-type-specific permissions, and Message Authentication Code security controls. He also integrated sanctions screening and risk-based approval rules into payment workflows.

For the global gateway, Matt designed message transformation, protocol translation, and routing across **12** countries, connecting ACCESS to clearing networks and internal compliance, settlement, and reporting systems. He led payments data migration, preserving client configurations, transaction histories, and regulatory traceability without disruption to live payment operations. ACCESS launched in **April 2011**; the gateway gave corporate treasurers a single entry point instead of separate regional logins.

At **American Express**, his work focused on adoption of the Virtual Payments Platform replacing a legacy mainframe system. Clients faced missing SDKs, fragmented documentation, no sandbox, and delayed testing. Matt led work on cross-stack SDKs, use-case-driven API documentation, a secure sandbox, and virtualized testing environments. He used Lean Product and XP practices and a Client Onboarding Test Drive to gather end-user feedback.

API consumption and trial usage increased within **3 months** of relaunch, and onboarding time decreased. The developer-portal team also exceeded its delivery commitment by over **261 story points** of customer-focused value.

## Turn 2. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes how he leads.

Teams Matt inherited were often “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, a rigid hierarchical culture initially made people slow to share ideas. Other teams avoided risks because they feared blame, slowing delivery and leaving opportunities unexplored.

Matt leads with “empathy, clarity, and modern engineering rigor.” He makes intent explicit, pushes decisions to the people closest to the work, and uses transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. He coaches teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks. Across a **150-person** Cloud Innovation Center, he grounded engineering culture in automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first practices.

His “permit to fail” approach included non-punitive post-mortems, transparent sharing of errors, and lean, low-stakes experiments. Teams applied lessons from post-mortems to subsequent sprints to prevent identified failure patterns from recurring. In early-stage product work, he set expectations that prototypes and user tests would likely fail—and that those results were evidence for deciding what to build next, rather than grounds for blame.

He also established weekly brainstorming sessions where ideas were considered without judgment, emphasized shared goals over individual accolades, and recognized contributions that advanced project goals. He brought product managers, designers, and engineers together in balanced teams, delivering thin-slice, production-ready solutions for rapid user feedback.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved **4x delivery acceleration** after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. Teams experimented more freely, repeat errors declined, and delivery timelines shortened. In Matt’s words, “It didn’t happen overnight, but it stuck.”

## Turn 3. Visitor: Give me an example.

### A

🐾 Here’s an example from **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center.

Matt’s cross-functional teams had a history of avoiding risks because they feared blame. That reluctance slowed delivery and caused missed opportunities to adopt modern engineering practices. As the centers scaled to **150+ practitioners**, many arriving from legacy command-and-control delivery cultures, some team members still hesitated to share ideas or take risks.

Matt introduced a “permit to fail” approach. Rather than pointing fingers when something went wrong, he organized non-punitive post-mortems to analyze mistakes and identify lessons learned. In team meetings and communications, he emphasized those lessons and encouraged transparent sharing of errors.

He paired that change with lean, low-stakes experiments. Teams tested hypotheses through controlled iterations instead of committing immediately to a larger change. The experiments gave engineers room to explore new technologies and approaches while keeping the work structured.

Matt also engaged regularly with team members to understand their challenges and aspirations. He conducted open retrospectives where people could voice concerns, and shared his own experiences with risk-taking and mistakes. The practice was not just permission to fail: teams applied post-mortem learnings to subsequent sprints to prevent identified failure patterns from recurring.

Teams began experimenting more freely. Repeat errors declined, and quicker pivots shortened project timelines. In the work addressing remaining pockets of fear of blame, team morale improved and turnover declined.
