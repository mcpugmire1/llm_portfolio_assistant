## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Yes—Matt describes a platform delivery where he relied on assumptions instead of verifying the environment his team was working in.

He was the tech lead on a global modernization engagement for a financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams running test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production. IAM controls, environment boundaries, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from earlier phases, along with documentation.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. Instead, he assumed the controls were correct and focused on delivery pressures. He did not audit who could trigger downstream email jobs, confirm that production customer data had been masked, or implement compensating controls such as email interception.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to **thousands** of real clients.

Matt took accountability and worked collectively with the team on root cause analysis and corrective controls. But that did not prevent an individual consequence: the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt describes it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

His stated change afterward was specific: IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership became day-one leadership responsibilities in his delivery approach. His wording for the failure is “assumptions are risks in disguise,” and his conclusion is that leaders own the environment their teams operate in.

### B

🐾 At **Norfolk Southern**, the balanced-team approach initially failed to prevent a quality crisis.

In early **2020**, Matt launched the Cloud Innovation Center’s first major engagement to modernize the client’s Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. The team emphasized test-driven development within a Lean Startup approach. A couple of months in, as the pandemic forced work remote, the client reported rising defects—even though dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The credibility hit landed in front of both the client and **Accenture** account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt found that an engineer was building from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story and writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete, but defects went undetected. The backlog also lacked defined scenarios and a definition of done. The balanced team and stated engineering practices had not ensured that the actual work followed those practices.

Matt took ownership of the failure rather than defending the metrics. He ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly, including the backlog problems. He rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work alongside the team and redo the code and tests. Coaching included boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and “I do, we do, you do” pairing.

Defect rates dropped, and the client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. The story reports restored client confidence, but no numerical defect reduction. Matt’s stated lesson was that metrics mean nothing without the practice behind them, and cultural transformation requires consistent coaching—not just process change.

### C

🐾 Yes—team autonomy at the Cloud Innovation Center didn’t work consistently as it scaled.

At **Accenture**, Matt designed the center to be flat from day one: autonomy at the team level, collective ownership, and minimal middle layers. But designing the model was not the same as living it. Many practitioners came from legacy delivery organizations where top-down, command-and-control patterns were the norm. Those habits reasserted themselves: decisions drifted upward, teams waited for direction, and bottlenecks formed where autonomy was supposed to be. Converting people to the model took enforcement and revalidation, multiple times; the path was bumpy.

The “permit to fail” culture also wasn’t universal. As the centers scaled to **150+ practitioners**, isolated pockets of fear of blame emerged. Some team members still hesitated to share ideas or take risks, limiting innovation and engagement in those areas.

Matt responded by giving cross-functional teams explicit decision-making authority and creating clear, flexible guidelines tied to project objectives. Anchors and team leads focused on removing obstacles rather than controlling outcomes. He used continuous retrospectives to revisit how teams were working, engaged directly with team members about their challenges, and shared his own experiences with risk-taking and mistakes rather than only asking others to be open.

The stories report fewer decision bottlenecks, higher engagement, improved morale, and reduced turnover after those changes. They do not give numerical measures for those improvements or describe a specific failed client delivery. The documented problem was that the intended working model repeatedly slipped back into centralized decisions and fear of blame—not that autonomy was established once and stayed in place.
