## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Yes—at **Norfolk Southern**, the balanced-team and test-driven development approach initially fell short.

In early **2020**, Matt launched the Cloud Innovation Center’s first major engagement: modernizing the Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months in, as the pandemic forced remote work, the client reported rising defects. The dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The problem reached both the client and **Accenture** account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt had emphasized test-driven development, but the team wasn’t consistently practicing it. An engineer was solutioning from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story and writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete while defects went undetected. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done.

Matt took ownership in front of the client and account leadership. Rather than defending the coverage metrics, he ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly—including the backlog problems. He rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work side by side with the team, redoing code and test cases.

He also introduced Samman-style coaching: boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and pairing through “I do, we do, you do.”

Defect rates dropped, and the client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Matt’s stated lesson was that “metrics mean nothing without the practice behind them,” and that cultural transformation requires consistent coaching, not just process change.

### D

🐾 At **Norfolk Southern**, Matt’s initial implementation of the team’s engineering practices did not hold.

Pair programming, test-first development, and continuous integration had been in place from the start, but the engagement still reached a quality crisis. Developers had resisted the new ways of working, the client was operating in mainframe thinking, and account leadership was accustomed to waterfall. The follow-up discipline needed to reinforce the practices had been missing. Standing them up was not the same as making them stick.

An audit and backlog rebuild followed, and TDD experts from Columbus paired side by side with the team to redo the work properly. Those experts were temporary. Then the pandemic removed co-location—the condition the pairing method had been built around.

Matt rebuilt the team’s day so that writing test cases was a discrete step before engineering the solution, done inside a pair and checked at standup. Instead of mandating compliance, he paired newer engineers with seasoned practitioners for repeated showing, doing, and reaffirming. Pairs rotated daily. Stories could enter sprints only when discrete and testable, with defined scenarios and a definition of done, capped at **3 points** within a **1-week** iteration. For remote work, pairs traded drivers frequently and pushed and pulled code before each trade.

The practices held after the experts left, and the failure mode that caused the crisis did not recur. Matt’s stated lesson was his own as much as the team’s: implementing a practice is the easy half; reinforcement, follow-up, and verification are what make it real.
