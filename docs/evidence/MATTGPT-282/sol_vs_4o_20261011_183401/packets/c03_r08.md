## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes—and practices—his leadership.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose: make intent explicit, push decisions to the people closest to the work, and coach teams to think clearly rather than just execute tasks. His leadership stories center on Org & Working-Model Transformation, Talent & Enablement, and Execution & Delivery.

At the **Accenture** Cloud Innovation Center, teams were designed for autonomy, but many practitioners arrived from command-and-control delivery cultures. Decisions drifted upward, teams waited for direction, and some people held back ideas for fear of blame. He brought product managers, designers, and engineers together with shared ownership and decision-making authority. Team leads focused on removing obstacles rather than controlling outcomes. He used clear decision-making guidelines, direct feedback, and continuous retrospectives. For people worried about making mistakes, he organized non-punitive post-mortems, shared his own experiences with mistakes, and supported low-stakes experiments—a “permit to fail” environment. Teams reported higher engagement and satisfaction; morale improved and turnover declined.

With client teams, Matt used “I do, we do, you do” coaching and **6-week** Agility Coaching Labs on their actual work. Moving from traditional IT delivery to self-regulating balanced teams reduced costs **15–35%**. His leadership account reports **4x** delivery acceleration once teams embraced lean product and modern engineering practices.

Autonomy still had boundaries: in financial services engagements, he embedded security requirements in the Definition of Done and made risk tradeoffs visible to leadership. Teams achieved **100%** compliance with PCI DSS, GDPR, and SOX. He also created hands-on learning material: NEW IT SKILLS NOW trained **300+** professionals, and AWS Launchpad produced **200+** AWS certifications.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

Those habits reasserted themselves: decisions drifted upward, teams waited for direction, and bottlenecks formed where autonomy was supposed to be. Matt’s account calls the path “bumpy” and says converting people to the model took “enforcement and revalidation, multiple times.”

Some team members still hesitated to share ideas or take risks, limiting innovation and engagement in those areas—even though openness had been part of the model from the beginning.

Matt responded with clear guidelines for decentralized decisions and team leads focused on removing obstacles rather than controlling outcomes. He also held open retrospectives, engaged directly with team members about their challenges, and shared personal experiences with risk-taking and mistakes.

The stories report fewer decision bottlenecks, improved morale, and reduced turnover afterward, but don’t quantify those changes. This is a documented example of the model not working consistently—not an account of an engagement that ultimately failed.
