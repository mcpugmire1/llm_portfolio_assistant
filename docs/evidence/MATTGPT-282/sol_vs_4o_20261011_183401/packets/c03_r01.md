## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 At **Norfolk Southern**, the initial rollout of his team’s engineering practices didn’t stick.

Pair programming, test-first development, and continuous integration had been in place from the start, but the engagement still reached a quality crisis. Developers had resisted the new ways of working, the client was operating in mainframe thinking, and account leadership was accustomed to waterfall. Matt’s account identifies missing reinforcement and follow-up: implementing the practices had not been enough to keep people using them.

The recovery included an audit, a rebuilt backlog, and TDD experts from Columbus pairing side by side with the team to redo the work properly. But those experts were temporary. Then the pandemic removed co-location—the condition the pairing method had been built around.

Matt rebuilt the team’s daily workflow so test cases came before engineering the solution, inside a pair, with progress checked at standup. Pairs rotated daily, and new engineers worked alongside seasoned practitioners. Stories entered sprints only when discrete and testable, with defined scenarios and a definition of done. They were capped at **3 points** to fit a **1-week** iteration. For remote work, pairs traded drivers frequently and pushed and pulled code before each trade.

The practices held after the experts left, and the failure mode that caused the crisis did not recur. Release cycles shortened and delivery became more predictable; the story does not quantify those improvements. Matt’s own lesson was that “implementing a practice is the easy half”—reinforcement, follow-up, and verification had been missing the first time.
