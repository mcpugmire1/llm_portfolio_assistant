## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Here's a great example.

During Matt's sabbatical in 2026, while building MattGPT, he encountered a significant challenge with his chatbot, Agy. The issue was that the regex only matched the exact phrase "Matt's ability," allowing rephrased versions to slip through. This was a recurring problem, with the evaluation suite failing **32.3%** of the time.

Matt's leadership style of addressing root causes rather than symptoms was crucial here. Instead of expanding the ban list, he diagnosed the real issue: conflicting instructions within the system prompt. He rebuilt the prompt from scratch, consolidating scattered instructions into a single, clear layer of rules. This structural change reduced evaluation suite failures to roughly **3-6%**. The fix was durable because it addressed the underlying cause rather than adding another patch.

This experience highlights that even with a strong leadership approach, challenges can arise, particularly when initial solutions don't address the root cause.

### B

🐾 Yes—at **Norfolk Southern**, the initial working model wasn’t producing the quality Matt expected.

On the Cloud Innovation Center’s first major engagement, modernizing the Revenue and Shipment Planning platform, Matt brought in a balanced team and emphasized test-driven development. A couple of months in, as the pandemic forced remote work, the client reported rising defects—even though the dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The failure landed in front of both the client and **Accenture** account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt took ownership rather than defending the metrics. A bottom-up audit found an engineer solutioning from his own interpretation instead of the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete, but defects went undetected. The backlog was also part of the problem: stories lacked defined scenarios and a definition of done.

The correction went beyond asking engineers to follow the process. Matt showed the gaps openly, rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories, and brought in frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work alongside the team. They redid the code and test cases. He introduced Samman-style coaching: boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and pairing with subject-matter experts through an “I do, we do, you do” progression. Product managers also received SMART story training.

Defect rates dropped, and the client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Client confidence was restored. The story records an initial failure and a subsequent correction—not an engagement that remained unsuccessful.
