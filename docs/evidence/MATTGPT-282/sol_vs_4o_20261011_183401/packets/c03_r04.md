### B

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes—and practices—his leadership style.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He makes intent explicit, pushes decisions to the people closest to the work, and coaches teams to think clearly—not just execute tasks. At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, the organization was designed flat from day one, but practitioners arriving from command-and-control environments sometimes reverted to waiting for direction. Keeping decisions with teams took repeated enforcement and revalidation; the path was bumpy.

He established balanced teams of product managers, designers, and engineers with shared ownership and decision-making authority. Leaders acted as facilitators, and team leads focused on removing obstacles rather than controlling outcomes. Autonomy came with clear guidelines, project objectives, continuous retrospectives, and engineering practices grounded in automation, continuous delivery, and quality.

His “permit to fail” environment included non-punitive post-mortems and low-stakes experiments. As the centers scaled to **150+ practitioners**, pockets of fear of blame persisted. Matt met with team members about their challenges and aspirations, shared his own experiences with mistakes, and used open retrospectives so people could raise concerns. Teams applied lessons to subsequent sprints, reduced recurring failure patterns, and reported improved morale; turnover declined.

With client teams, he used “I do, we do, you do” coaching and immersive **6-week** Agility Coaching Labs on their actual work. Operating-model engagements reduced costs **15–35%**. He also made security tradeoffs visible to client leadership and embedded security requirements in the Definition of Done; those engagements achieved **100%** compliance with PCI DSS, GDPR, and SOX.

At the CIC, teams achieved **4x delivery acceleration** after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. His broader enablement work trained **300+ professionals** through NEW IT SKILLS NOW; AWS Launchpad produced **200+ AWS certifications**.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Here's a great example.

At **Norfolk Southern**, Matt faced a significant challenge when his leadership approach met resistance. The Cloud Innovation Center's first major engagement with **Norfolk Southern** involved modernizing their legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. Despite emphasizing test-driven development and achieving near-complete test coverage, the client raised concerns about a rising number of defects. This feedback caught Matt off guard, as the metrics suggested everything was on track. The credibility hit was significant, especially since the **Accenture** account leadership team was accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt took ownership of the situation and dug into the root causes instead of defending the metrics. He discovered that an engineer was interpreting stories from his own perspective rather than following the backlog, writing tests after the code, which made the coverage look complete while defects went undetected. To address this, Matt ran a complete audit of the codebase and test suite, openly showing the gaps and rebuilding the backlog with SMART, testable stories. He brought in TDD experts to coach the team and restructured agile rituals to maintain alignment among stakeholders, developers, and product managers.

As a result, the client stopped finding defects that the dashboards couldn't see, and defect rates dropped as story quality and test-first discipline took hold. Client confidence was restored through transparency and visible improvement, demonstrating that cultural transformation requires consistent coaching, not just process change. This experience reinforced the importance of aligning metrics with genuine practices and maintaining open communication with clients and teams.

### B

🐾 One example is when team autonomy didn’t work as intended at **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center.

Matt designed the organization flat from day one: autonomy at the team level, collective ownership, and minimal middle layers. But many practitioners arrived from legacy delivery organizations where top-down, command-and-control patterns were the norm. Those habits reasserted themselves: decisions drifted upward, teams waited for direction, and bottlenecks formed where autonomy was supposed to be. Establishing the model had not made it the way people actually worked. Converting people to it took “enforcement and revalidation, multiple times,” and the path was bumpy.

Matt responded by giving product managers, designers, and engineers shared decision-making authority, with clear, flexible guidelines tied to project objectives. Team leads focused on removing obstacles rather than controlling outcomes. Continuous retrospectives gave teams opportunities to adjust how they worked.

There was a related problem as the centers scaled to **150+ practitioners**: some team members still feared blame and hesitated to share ideas or take risks, despite the culture of openness. Matt met with people about their challenges, held open retrospectives, and shared his own experiences with mistakes.

The reported outcomes were fewer decision bottlenecks, higher engagement, improved morale, and reduced turnover. These accounts describe an approach that needed repeated correction—not an outright failed program. They don’t quantify the delays or turnover caused while those problems persisted.
