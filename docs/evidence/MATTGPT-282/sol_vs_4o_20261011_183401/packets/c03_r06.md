## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 At **Norfolk Southern**, the practices Matt introduced didn’t stick at first.

Pair programming, test-first development, and continuous integration had been in place from day one. But developers had resisted the new ways of working, the client was operating in mainframe thinking, and account leadership was accustomed to waterfall. A quality crisis followed. An audit, a rebuilt backlog, and TDD experts from Columbus pairing side by side with the team were needed to redo the work properly.

Matt’s account explicitly identifies what had been missing: reinforcement, follow-up, and verification. Implementing the practices wasn’t enough. The outside experts were temporary, and the pandemic then removed the co-location the method was built around.

Matt rebuilt the team’s day so test cases became a discrete step before engineering the solution, inside a pair, checked at standup. Rather than mandating compliance, he paired newer engineers with seasoned practitioners for “showing, doing, and reaffirming” through repeated iterations. Pairs rotated daily, each pulling a single story from the prioritized backlog. Stories entered sprints only when discrete and testable, with defined scenarios and a definition of done, capped at **3 points** within a **1-week** iteration. For remote work, pairs traded drivers frequently, pushing and pulling code before each trade.

The practices held after the experts left, and the failure mode that caused the crisis did not recur. Matt’s stated lesson was his as much as the team’s: “implementing a practice is the easy half, and reinforcement, follow-up, and verification are what make it real.”

### B

🐾 Here's a great example.

During Matt's sabbatical in 2026, while working on his independent project MattGPT, he encountered a situation where his leadership approach initially didn't work as intended. The chatbot, Agy, was generating evaluative language despite having a post-processing filter designed to prevent it. The filter was supposed to catch phrases like "Matt's ability," but the model was rephrasing to "His ability," slipping past the filter. This led to a failure rate of **32.3%** in the evaluation suite.

Matt's initial approach of using a regex-based post-processing step wasn't holding up. Instead of expanding the ban list, he diagnosed the root cause as conflicting instructions within the system prompt. To address this, he rebuilt the prompt architecture from scratch, consolidating instructions into a single layer of rules. This structural fix reduced the failure rate to roughly **3-6%**, demonstrating that addressing the underlying issue was more effective than patching symptoms.
