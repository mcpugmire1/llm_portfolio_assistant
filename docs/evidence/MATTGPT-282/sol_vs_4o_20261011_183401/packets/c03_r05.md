# Conversation 3, run 5

## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Matt describes his leadership approach as empathy, clarity, and modern engineering rigor: make intent explicit, push decisions to the people closest to the work, and coach teams to think clearly—not just execute tasks.

At **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center, the organization was designed flat from day one, with team autonomy and collective ownership. Living that model was harder. As the centers scaled to **150+ practitioners**, people arriving from command-and-control delivery cultures sometimes waited for direction, pushed decisions upward, or hesitated to share mistakes. Matt had to reinforce and revalidate the model repeatedly.

He established balanced teams of product managers, designers, and engineers with shared decision-making authority. Leaders acted as facilitators; team leads removed obstacles rather than controlled outcomes. Matt used direct feedback, open retrospectives, and conversations about team members’ challenges and aspirations. He shared his own experiences with mistakes and introduced a “permit to fail” environment through non-punitive post-mortems and low-stakes experiments. Teams applied lessons to subsequent sprints, preventing recurring failure patterns; turnover declined and delivery timelines shortened.

His coaching used “I do, we do, you do,” including **6-week** Agility Coaching Labs on clients’ actual work. Clients moving from traditional IT delivery to self-regulating balanced teams reduced costs **15–35%**. At the CIC, teams achieved **4x delivery acceleration** after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices.

Autonomy still had explicit requirements: security checklists were part of the Definition of Done, and teams could not deploy without meeting minimum security requirements. Those engagements achieved **100% compliance** with PCI DSS, GDPR, and SOX. Matt also wrote and taught material teams could use beyond an engagement: NEW IT SKILLS NOW trained **300+ professionals**, while **RBC** adopted his data-migration strategy and **Fiserv** adopted accessibility standards as ongoing development practice.

### B

🐾 Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He creates a "permit to fail" learning environment, which encourages innovation and growth among his teams. His leadership philosophy includes balancing teams by integrating Product, Engineering, and Design together to foster a collaborative and effective working atmosphere.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 The closest documented example is a scaling problem at **Accenture’s** Cloud Innovation Center—not a documented case where his leadership approach ultimately failed.

As the center scaled, teams spent valuable time reinventing solutions or implementing unique patterns for problems that other teams had already solved. That fragmented approach produced misaligned architectures, slowed delivery timelines, and increased project costs. The shared practices needed to support consistent modernization were not keeping pace with growth. This was happening inside the organization Matt was building, rather than merely being a problem he inherited at a client.

Matt changed how teams accessed and reused solutions. He built a centralized repository in Git and Confluence, documenting patterns for moving monoliths to microservices, managing dependencies, and implementing CI/CD pipelines. Instead of leaving those solutions within individual teams, he organized weekly workshops where teams presented their work and contributed to the library. He also embedded reusable patterns into delivery toolchains and appointed a cross-functional review board to maintain and evolve them.

Teams subsequently adopted pre-approved patterns, accelerated project initiation, and improved architectural consistency. The account does not quantify the earlier cost increase or delivery delays, and it does not establish that pushing decisions closer to the work caused them. It documents a concrete problem during scaling and the additional shared infrastructure Matt introduced in response—not an outright failure of his leadership style.

### B

🐾 Here's a great example.

At **Norfolk Southern**, Matt faced a significant challenge when his "permit to fail" culture initially did not yield the expected results. During the modernization of their Revenue and Shipment Planning platform, the client raised concerns about rising defects despite near-complete test coverage. The issue was traced back to an engineer who was interpreting requirements on his own and writing tests after the code, which made the coverage appear complete while defects went undetected. This situation occurred in the early months of 2020, right as the pandemic shifted everything to remote work.

Instead of defending the metrics, Matt took ownership of the failure in front of both the client and **Accenture** account leadership. He conducted a thorough audit of the codebase and test suite, revealing that the backlog's vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done left room for misinterpretation. Matt addressed this by rebuilding the backlog with SMART, testable stories, and implementing a hands-on coaching approach using the Samman style. This included boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and pairing engineers with subject matter experts.

The result was a significant improvement in story quality and test-first discipline, leading to a drop in defect rates and restored client confidence. The team learned that metrics like test coverage mean nothing without the practice behind them, and the experience reinforced that cultural transformation requires consistent coaching, not just process change.
