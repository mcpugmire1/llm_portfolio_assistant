## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt leads.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center, teams also faced role silos and fear of blame. Engineers hesitated to experiment, while hierarchical structures and handoffs slowed delivery.

Matt made intent explicit and pushed decisions to the people closest to the work. He used transparency, direct feedback, and open communication, coaching teams to think clearly rather than just execute tasks. He assembled balanced teams of engineers, product managers, and UX architects with equal participation and shared accountability, instead of keeping those roles in separate silos.

His “permit to fail” environment had specific practices behind it: non-punitive post-mortems, transparent sharing of errors, and lean, low-stakes experiments with structured hypotheses and controlled iterations. Teams applied lessons from mistakes to subsequent sprints rather than pointing fingers.

For innovation work, Matt set expectations upfront that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail, intentionally. Teams used user research, Jobs to Be Done framing, and rapid testing to challenge assumptions before committing to costly builds. Failed experiments became decision inputs that shaped product direction week by week.

He paired that room to experiment with engineering rigor: automation, continuous delivery, quality-first practices, pair programming, and collaborative coding. At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. The balanced-team work also reduced handoffs and project delays and achieved zero-defect production code through iterative development and real-time feedback loops.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Yes. At Norfolk Southern, the balanced-team setup and emphasis on test-driven development did not initially prevent a quality failure.

In early 2020, Matt launched the Cloud Innovation Center’s first major engagement, modernizing Norfolk Southern’s legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months in, as the pandemic pushed everyone into remote work, the client reported rising defects. The dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The feedback caught the team off guard, and the credibility hit landed in front of both the client and Accenture account leadership.

Matt found that an engineer was building from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete while defects went undetected. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done.

Matt took ownership in front of the client and account leadership rather than defending the metrics. He ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly, including the problems in the backlog.

He rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work side by side with the team. They redid the code and test cases. Coaching followed an “I do, we do, you do” progression, supported by boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and pairing with subject-matter experts.

Defect rates dropped, and the client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Matt’s stated lesson was that metrics mean nothing without the practice behind them, and cultural transformation requires consistent coaching, not just process change.

### B

🐾 There’s an example where Matt’s “permit to fail” principle did not protect someone from consequences.

On a global platform modernization for a financial services client, Matt was the tech lead responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams running test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production. He relied on inherited documentation and assumed the identity and access management controls, environment boundaries, and downstream access configurations were correct.

He did not verify those assumptions. He did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes, confirm that production customer data had been masked, or implement compensating controls such as email interception.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. He worked collectively with the team to understand what happened and implement corrective controls. But the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. In Matt’s words, it was “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

His stated change afterward was specific: IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership became “day-one leadership responsibilities” in his delivery approach. His account does not claim that taking accountability reversed the tester’s removal.
