He built balanced teams with engineers, product managers, and UX architects sharing accountability. Instead of routing decisions through management layers, teams held execution power, practiced pair programming, and used retrospectives to change how they worked. Engineers, designers, and product managers could voice concerns and implement changes autonomously. Across the leadership-philosophy story, teams accelerated delivery 4x after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices.

Matt also owned hiring, performance feedback, promotions, compensation, and remediation. Because managers did not sit on daily teams, he built evaluations around evidence from recent pair partners, product managers, and team anchors, rather than visibility to a boss. Practice leads reserved Friday afternoons for career-development conversations.

The CIC grew to 150+ practitioners, with near-zero attrition in the first two years. Its deliberately flat structure kept decisions with teams while Matt retained responsibility for final hiring decisions, compensation allocation, and end-of-remediation calls.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 There’s a case where Matt’s own account is explicit about what he got wrong.

On a global platform modernization for a financial services client, Matt was the tech lead responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams running tests across development, QA, UAT, and production. He relied on inherited documentation rather than verifying that access controls and environment boundaries were correct.

Matt focused on delivery pressures instead of validating access patterns. He did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes, implement data masking or email interception, or verify whether production customer data had been scrubbed from downstream environments.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. But the non-punitive response he advocates did not carry through: the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. His account calls it “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

This is not an example of team autonomy itself failing. It is an example of Matt not verifying the environment his team depended on, and a team member bearing a consequence. He subsequently made IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership “day-one leadership responsibilities” in his delivery approach.

### B

🐾 Yes. The Norfolk Southern example I mentioned was initially a failure, not a clean success.

On the Cloud Innovation Center’s first major engagement, Matt brought in a balanced team and emphasized test-driven development. But a couple of months into modernizing the Revenue and Shipment Planning platform, the client reported rising defects while dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The credibility hit landed in front of both the client and Accenture account leadership, just as the pandemic pushed the team into remote work.

The practices had been in place from the start, but the reinforcement and verification were missing. An engineer was solutioning from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story and writing tests after the code. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done. Matt had introduced the working model, but it was not being followed consistently, and the dashboards were not exposing that.

Matt took ownership in front of the client and account leadership. He audited the codebase and tests, showed the gaps openly, rebuilt the backlog, and brought in frontend and backend TDD experts to redo the work alongside the team. After that intervention, he changed the daily workflow: test cases came before the solution, pairs checked that discipline at standup, and newer engineers worked with seasoned practitioners rather than relying on training alone.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. The practices held after the experts left, and the crisis failure mode did not recur. Matt’s own account is explicit: implementing a practice was the easy half; reinforcement, follow-up, and verification had been missing.
