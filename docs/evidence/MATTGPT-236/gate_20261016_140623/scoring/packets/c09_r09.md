## Turn 3. Visitor: Give me an example.

### A

🐾 Here’s an example from Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center.

The team had a history of avoiding risks because people feared blame. That reluctance meant missed opportunities to adopt modern engineering practices, less experimentation, and slower delivery. Matt’s task was to establish a “permit to fail” mindset, where mistakes became learning opportunities rather than reasons to point fingers.

He organized non-punitive post-mortems so the team could analyze mistakes and share errors transparently. In team meetings and communications, he emphasized the lessons learned rather than who was responsible for the failure. The work did not stop at discussing what went wrong: teams applied the post-mortem findings to subsequent sprints to prevent the same failure patterns from recurring.

Matt also supported lean, low-stakes experiments. Instead of asking teams to commit to an untested approach, he encouraged structured hypotheses and controlled iterations. Engineers could test new technologies and approaches, examine what happened, and change direction before making a larger commitment.

Team members began experimenting more freely. Applying lessons from post-mortems prevented identified failure patterns from recurring and improved reliability. The lean experiments also enabled quicker pivots and shorter project timelines.

Those outcomes were not quantified in this particular story. The reported changes were fewer repeat errors, more experimentation, and shorter delivery timelines across the Cloud Innovation Center’s cross-functional teams.

### B

🐾 Here’s a specific example from Norfolk Southern.

In 2020, Matt’s first major Cloud Innovation Center engagement was modernizing the client’s legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months in, just as the pandemic forced the team into remote work, the client reported rising defects. The dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The concerns landed in front of both the client and Accenture account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt took ownership of the failure rather than defending the metrics. He ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly. An engineer had been working from his own interpretation instead of the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete, but defects went undetected. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done.

Matt rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories. He brought frontend and backend test-driven development experts from Columbus to work side by side with the team, then had the code and test cases redone properly. Coaching included boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and pairing engineers with subject-matter experts through an “I do, we do, you do” progression. He also restructured agile ceremonies and delivered product-management and SMART-story training.

Defect rates dropped as story quality and test-first discipline took hold. The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see, and confidence was restored through the open audit and visible improvement. The story does not give a numerical defect reduction.
