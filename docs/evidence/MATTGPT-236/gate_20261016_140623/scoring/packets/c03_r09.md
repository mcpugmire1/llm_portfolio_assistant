## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 There’s an example where Matt did not ensure a safe environment for his team.

On a global platform modernization for a financial services client, Matt was the tech lead across development, QA, UAT, and production environments. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams executing test cycles. IAM controls, environment boundaries, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from prior phases.

Matt relied on that documentation rather than verifying it. He focused on delivery pressures instead of validating access patterns, did not audit who could trigger downstream email processes, and did not confirm that production customer names and email addresses had been masked. Compensating controls, including data masking and email interception, were not in place.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. But the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. In Matt’s words, it was “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

This was not a low-stakes experiment within his “permit to fail” approach. Matt had not verified the controls needed to make the environment safe, and the tester faced the consequence. Afterward, he made IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership “day-one leadership responsibilities” in his delivery approach.

### B

🐾 Yes. The Norfolk Southern example I mentioned started with a failure of the working practices Matt had introduced.

On the Cloud Innovation Center’s first major engagement, modernizing the legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform, Matt brought in a balanced team and emphasized test-driven development. A couple of months in, as the pandemic forced work remote, the client reported rising defects despite near-complete test coverage. The credibility hit landed in front of both the client and Accenture account leadership.

Pairing, test-first development, and continuous integration had been in place from the start, but the reinforcement and verification were missing. An engineer was building from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story and writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete while defects went undetected. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done.

Matt took ownership rather than defending the dashboard. He audited the codebase and tests, exposed the backlog gaps, and brought in frontend and backend TDD experts to redo the work alongside engineers. He then changed the daily workflow: test cases came before the solution, inside rotating pairs, with the sequence checked at standup. New engineers paired with seasoned practitioners. Stories entered sprints only when discrete and testable, small enough for a one-week iteration.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see, and the failure mode did not recur after the experts left. Matt’s stated lesson was that implementing a practice is the easy half; reinforcement, follow-up, and verification are what make it real.
