## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt leads.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” His response was to make intent explicit, push decisions to the people closest to the work, and coach teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks.

At Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center, some team members avoided risks because they feared blame. Matt introduced a “permit to fail” environment through non-punitive post-mortems and lean, low-stakes experiments. Instead of pointing fingers, teams examined mistakes, shared lessons, and applied those lessons in subsequent sprints. People began experimenting more freely, and identified failure patterns stopped recurring.

He also replaced role silos with balanced teams of engineers, product managers, and UX architects, with equal participation and shared accountability. Pair programming, collaborative coding, and regular retrospectives gave teammates ways to share knowledge, surface roadblocks, and change how they worked without waiting for management approval.

In an organization where managers did not sit on daily teams, Matt built evaluations around evidence from recent pairing partners, product managers, and team anchors, rather than visibility to a boss. He established career-level expectations, facilitated promotion calibration, allocated compensation, and owned final hiring and end-of-remediation decisions. Friday afternoons included career-development conversations with practice leads.

The Cloud Innovation Center grew to 150+ practitioners, with near-zero attrition in its first 2 years. His leadership-philosophy account reports 4x delivery acceleration once teams embraced lean product and modern engineering practices.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Yes. At Norfolk Southern, the balanced-team model and emphasis on test-driven development did not initially produce the quality Matt expected.

In early 2020, the Cloud Innovation Center began modernizing the client’s legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months in, as the pandemic forced everyone into remote work, the client reported rising defects. The team’s dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The discrepancy caught Matt off guard, and the credibility hit landed in front of both the client and Accenture account leadership.

Matt found that an engineer had been building from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete, but defects went undetected. The backlog also lacked defined scenarios and a definition of done, leaving engineers without clear requirements to test against.

Matt took ownership in front of the client and account leadership rather than defending the metrics. He ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly, including the problems in the backlog.

He then rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought in frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work alongside the team. Engineers redid the code and test cases, supported by boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and pairing through an “I do, we do, you do” progression.

Defect rates dropped, and Norfolk Southern stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. The initial setup had left engineers working from vague stories while leadership relied on coverage numbers. Matt changed both the requirements and the day-to-day coaching, not just the reporting.
