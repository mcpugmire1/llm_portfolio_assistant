## Turn 1. Visitor: Tell me about a time he failed.

### A

🐾 Here’s a time Matt failed.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization delivery for a financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams executing test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production. IAM controls, environment segregation, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from prior phases, along with existing documentation.

His responsibility was to ensure a safe environment setup. Instead, he relied on that documentation without confirming its accuracy. He focused on delivery pressures rather than validating access patterns, did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes, and did not verify whether production customer names and email addresses had been masked or scrubbed. He also had not implemented compensating controls such as data masking or email interception.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. They traced the incident to insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed from downstream environments. He worked with the team to understand what happened and implement corrective controls, rather than treating the tester’s action as the whole explanation.

The client nevertheless demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.” Afterward, IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership became day-one responsibilities in his delivery approach. His own description of the mistake was direct: these were “assumptions [he] should have verified rather than inherited.”
