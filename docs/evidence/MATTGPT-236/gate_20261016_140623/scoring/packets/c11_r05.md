## Turn 1. Visitor: How did Matt scale engineering teams from 4 to 150+ people?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt built and ran that organization.

The CIC stories describe growth from a 10-person pilot to 150+ practitioners, rather than a 4-person starting point. At Accenture, Matt built the Atlanta and Tampa Cloud Innovation Centers as demand for cloud-native delivery grew. Many new hires lacked hands-on cloud-native or Agile experience. The organization included engineering, architecture, product, and design, so scaling meant establishing shared practices as well as hiring people.

Matt built a hiring engine across bench searches, campus recruiting, contract-to-hire, and approved job requisitions. Engineers completed hands-on TDD pairing auditions; product managers and designers worked through live scoping exercises. Matt held the closing interview and made the final decision on every offer. During scaling pushes, the process produced 4 to 6 net-new practitioners per week, with interview participation capped at 2 slots per practitioner per week to protect delivery time.

New hires joined tailored enablement programs, then paired with experienced practitioners. Training covered pair programming, TDD, CI/CD, lean product management, and user-centered design. The learning progression was “I do, we do, you do,” moving practitioners from guided work to independent delivery. Weekly peer-led demos, practice forums, skills matrices, and career development conversations continued after onboarding.

Matt kept the organization deliberately flat: 11 to 13 practice and client leads reported directly to him. Experienced engineering “anchors” worked inside balanced teams without adding another management layer. Monday allocation reviews assigned practitioners across client engagements, training, and accelerator development. Because managers did not sit on daily teams, evaluations drew on evidence from recent pairing partners, product managers, and anchors.

He also corrected practices when delivery exposed gaps. At Norfolk Southern, rising defects prompted a code-and-test audit, rewritten backlog stories, and hands-on TDD coaching rather than defending test-coverage dashboards.

Over 4 years, the centers reached 150+ practitioners, with near-zero attrition in the first 2 years. Across CIC delivery, 10-person balanced teams delivered output equivalent to traditional 20-person teams, with reported 4X faster velocity and zero-defect deployments.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Tell me about his payments work

### A

🐾 Here’s Matt’s payments work, from payment engines and global gateways to developer integration.

At JP Morgan Chase, Matt worked on ACCESS Next Generation from 2009 to 2012. The bank’s regional payment systems had different data models, entitlements, message formats, and compliance requirements. Bottomline Technologies’ WebSeries platform provided the foundation, but its out-of-box capabilities did not cover the requirements of 135,000+ clients across 180+ countries.

As Technology Delivery Lead, Matt managed a blended team of 40+ Accenture and bank practitioners across the US, Mumbai, and Bangalore. He designed and delivered payment modules for ACH credits and direct debits, Fedwire, international wires, FX/cross-border payments, and federal tax payments. The work included NACHA file validation, payment-type-specific permissions, Message Authentication Code security, sanctions screening, and integration with the bank’s transaction monitoring systems.

He also designed the centralized payments gateway: message transformation, protocol translation, and routing across 12 countries. Instead of separate regional integrations and logins, corporate treasurers received a single entry point for initiating payments. Matt led the payments-workstream data migration, preserving client configurations, entitlement structures, transaction histories, and compliance traceability without disruption to live payment operations. ACCESS launched in April 2011. His responsibilities continued into production support and critical-incident response.

At American Express, his work on the Virtual Payments Platform addressed a different problem: clients struggled to evaluate and integrate the replacement for a legacy mainframe system. SDKs were missing, documentation was fragmented, and there was no sandbox.

Matt led work on a developer portal, Java, .NET, and Python SDKs, use-case-driven API documentation, and a secure sandbox. Environment virtualization reduced downstream dependencies and enabled earlier testing. API consumption and trial usage increased within 3 months of relaunch, onboarding time decreased, and the team delivered over 261 additional story points beyond its delivery commitment.
