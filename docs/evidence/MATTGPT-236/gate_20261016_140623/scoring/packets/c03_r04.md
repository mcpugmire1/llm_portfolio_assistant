The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Across his Cloud Innovation Center leadership work, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration once they embraced lean product and modern engineering practices.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Yes. In one financial services engagement, Matt’s failure to verify the team’s operating environment had consequences for both customers and a tester.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization delivery. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams running test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production environments. IAM controls, environment boundaries, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from earlier phases. His responsibility was to ensure a safe environment setup, but he relied on the existing documentation rather than confirming that it was accurate.

He focused on delivery pressures instead of validating access patterns. He did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes, implement data masking or email interception, or verify whether production customer names and email addresses had been scrubbed from downstream environments.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. Communications containing unmasked production customer data went to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. But that did not protect the tester from the consequence: the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

This was not a controlled, low-stakes experiment within a “permit to fail” environment. Matt had assumed safeguards were in place without verifying them. Afterward, he made IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership “day-one leadership responsibilities” in his delivery approach.

### B

🐾 Yes. At Norfolk Southern, the balanced-team model and emphasis on test-driven development did not initially prevent a quality failure.

In early 2020, Matt’s Cloud Innovation Center team was modernizing the client’s Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months into the engagement, as the pandemic forced work fully remote, the client reported rising defects. The team’s dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The feedback caught Matt off guard, and the credibility hit landed in front of both the client and Accenture account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt took ownership rather than defending the metrics. A bottom-up audit found that an engineer was building from his own interpretation instead of the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete while defects went undetected. The backlog was also part of the problem: stories lacked defined scenarios and a definition of done.

Matt showed those gaps openly. He rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work side by side with the team. They redid the code and test cases, then used boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and an “I do, we do, you do” coaching progression. He also restructured agile ceremonies to bring stakeholders, developers, and product managers together around the requirements.

Defect rates dropped, and the client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Client and account leadership confidence returned through the open audit and visible improvement. Matt’s stated lesson was that cultural transformation requires consistent coaching, not just process change.
