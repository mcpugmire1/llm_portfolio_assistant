## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt leads.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center, teams also faced hierarchical structures and role silos that slowed delivery. Some avoided risks because they feared blame; others entered innovation work expecting polished requirements and predictable outcomes when they actually had assumptions to test.

Rather than add more approval steps, Matt pushed decisions to the people closest to the work. He made intent explicit, used transparency and direct feedback, and coached teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks. He assembled balanced teams of engineers, product managers, and UX architects with equal participation and shared accountability. Pair programming, collaborative coding, stand-ups, and retrospectives became part of daily work, with teams able to voice feedback and implement changes autonomously.

His “permit to fail” environment included non-punitive post-mortems and lean, low-stakes experiments. Instead of pointing fingers, teams analyzed mistakes and applied the lessons in subsequent sprints. In early-stage product work, Matt set expectations upfront that prototypes and user tests would likely fail. He used user research, JTBD framing, and rapid testing to validate assumptions before committing to costly builds. Teams delivered thin-slice, production-ready solutions to get user feedback rather than wait for a complete product.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. The balanced-team work also reduced handoffs and project delays and achieved zero-defect production code through iterative development and real-time feedback loops.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 There’s a failure Matt explicitly takes responsibility for.

On a global platform modernization for a Fortune 500 financial services client, Matt was the tech lead responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams running test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production. Access controls and environment configurations had been inherited from earlier phases, along with documentation.

Matt relied on that documentation instead of verifying the configuration. He focused on delivery pressures without confirming who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes. He also did not verify whether production customer data had been masked or scrubbed, or implement compensating controls such as email interception.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and brought the team together for root cause analysis and remediation. They identified insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed from downstream environments. However, the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt describes it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

This was not a low-stakes experiment within his “permit to fail” approach. Matt had failed to verify the environment his team was operating in, and the tester still faced the consequence. Afterward, he made IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership “day-one leadership responsibilities” in his delivery approach.

### B

🐾 At Norfolk Southern, the initial approach did not prevent a quality crisis.

In early 2020, Matt launched the Cloud Innovation Center’s first major engagement to modernize Norfolk Southern’s Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. He brought in a balanced team and emphasized test-driven development. A couple of months in, just as the pandemic forced remote work, the client reported rising defects despite dashboards showing near-complete test coverage. The credibility hit landed in front of both the client and Accenture account leadership.

Matt found that an engineer was solutioning from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete while defects went undetected. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done, leaving no clear way to test them.

Rather than defend the metrics, Matt took ownership of the failure and ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite. He showed the gaps openly, rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories, and brought in frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to redo code and tests alongside the team.

The correction required more than stating the engineering practices. Matt introduced Samman-style coaching: boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and pairing with experts through an “I do, we do, you do” progression. He also restructured agile ceremonies and added product management and SMART story training.

Defect rates dropped, and the client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Matt’s stated lesson was that cultural transformation requires consistent coaching, not just process change.
