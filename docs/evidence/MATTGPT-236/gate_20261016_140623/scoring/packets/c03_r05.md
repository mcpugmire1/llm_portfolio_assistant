## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership: empathy, clarity, and purpose.

Most engineering teams he inherited were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At the Accenture Cloud Innovation Center, teams also faced hierarchical structures and role silos that slowed delivery. Some avoided risks because they feared blame. Matt focused on changing those conditions rather than adding more process.

He made intent explicit, pushed decisions to the people closest to the work, and used transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. He coached teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks. Engineers, product managers, and UX architects worked in balanced teams with equal participation and shared accountability, rather than handing work from one function to another. Daily practices included pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and knowledge-sharing sessions.

His “permit to fail” environment had specific routines: non-punitive post-mortems, transparent sharing of mistakes, and lean, low-stakes experiments with structured hypotheses and controlled iterations. In product incubation, he set expectations upfront that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail. Teams used user research, Jobs to Be Done framing, and rapid testing to validate assumptions before committing to costly builds. Failed experiments became decision inputs, not occasions for pointing fingers.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, his leadership approach produced 4x delivery acceleration once teams embraced lean product and modern engineering practices. The balanced-team work also reduced handoffs and project delays, improved retention, and achieved zero-defect production code through iterative development and real-time feedback loops.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 At Norfolk Southern, Matt’s initial rollout of balanced teams and test-driven development did not prevent a quality crisis.

In early 2020, the Cloud Innovation Center’s first major engagement was modernizing the client’s legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months in, as the pandemic forced the team into remote work, the client reported rising defects. The dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The failure landed in front of both the client and Accenture account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery.

Matt took ownership rather than defending the metrics. A bottom-up audit found that an engineer was solutioning from his own interpretation instead of the backlog story and writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete while defects went undetected. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done.

Matt showed those gaps openly. He rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought in frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work side by side with the team. They redid the code and test cases, then used boot camps, coding katas, practice learning sessions, and an “I do, we do, you do” coaching progression. He also restructured agile ceremonies and provided product management training.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Defect rates dropped, and client confidence was restored through the audit’s openness and visible improvement. Matt’s stated lesson was that cultural transformation requires consistent coaching, not just process change.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization for a financial services client.
