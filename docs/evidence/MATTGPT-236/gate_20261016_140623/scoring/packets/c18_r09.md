## Turn 1. Visitor: Tell me about his Norfolk Southern work.

### A

🐾 Here’s the story of Matt’s work at Norfolk Southern.

Matt led the Cloud Innovation Center’s first engagement: modernizing the railroad’s Revenue and Shipment Planning platform without stopping the railroad. Its mainframe dated to the 1970s. Orders could fall out unnoticed until customers called, business rules lived in COBOL and assembly maintained by a shrinking workforce, and clerks manually shepherded waybill and rating fallout through green-screen consoles. The client organization was shaped around mainframe thinking, account leadership expected waterfall delivery, and Matt’s team was learning a new working model as COVID forced remote work.

Matt led domain-driven design and event-storming workshops to map business functions and dependencies. The mainframe remained the system of record while APIs, microservices, and CockroachDB rose beside it, with change data capture and reconciliation keeping the platforms synchronized. The architecture work also included the Boris Exercise, SNAP-E documentation, and anti-corruption layers for legacy integration. When the client grew restless for tangible progress, he brought revenue management staff into the innovation hub to co-create the operational workspace through personas, prototypes, and weekly build-measure-learn iterations.

The engagement also hit a quality crisis: defects were rising despite near-complete test coverage. Matt took ownership, audited the code and tests, and found tests written after code alongside vague backlog stories. He brought in TDD experts, rebuilt testable stories, and reinforced test-first work through rotating pairs and daily checks. The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see, and that failure mode did not recur.

For employees, pairing spread scarce legacy knowledge beyond thin-stretched experts. A 2-day CIC Academy course ran twice monthly over 6 months, while client contributors learned Angular, Node, Spring Boot, and cloud-native concepts to support the application themselves.

The SRS UI MVP launched covering 95% of its targeted use cases. Billing clerks and shippers could audit malformed waybill data before downstream pricing errors, while EDI validation moved fallout detection from customer complaints to proactive trading-partner notifications.

From June 2012 to June 2013, Matt worked as a Senior Technology Architecture Manager on commercial banking modernization. Leadership needed to determine whether the proposed platform could deliver what the business required before committing further investment.

Matt conducted an architecture assessment and gap/fit analysis, drawing on prior experience implementing the same platform. He created architectural heat maps and worked with the enterprise architecture team on target-system models and a transparent cost-to-fix versus benefit comparison. The assessment found that WebSeries lacked the modularity required for RBC’s long-term digital operating model. Rather than proceeding with the proposed platform, RBC redirected critical technical resources toward higher-priority initiatives before significant spend was committed.

For mobile-first planning, Matt defined API integration points, cheque deposit requirements, cross-browser and cross-device compatibility, and platform scalability needs. He delivered a modernization blueprint, not a claim of a launched mobile application. He also published and presented a legacy commercial banking data migration strategy to senior leadership, which RBC adopted.

Alongside the architecture work, Matt established the PMO across RBC, Accenture, and Bottomline Technologies. He introduced centralized updates, designated team contacts, a task-tracking matrix, and risk and dependency reporting. The communication framework eliminated missed program updates, while the matrix reduced redundant efforts and clarified resource assignments.
