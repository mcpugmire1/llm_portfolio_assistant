## Turn 1. Visitor: How did Matt scale engineering teams from 4 to 150+ people?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt built the organization.

At Accenture, Matt built the Atlanta and Tampa Cloud Innovation Centers from the ground up. The documented starting point was a 10-person pilot, rather than 4 people, growing to 150+ practitioners across engineering, architecture, product, and design over four years. Many new hires lacked hands-on cloud-native or Agile experience, so expanding headcount also meant building training, career development, and a management model that did not depend on adding layers.

Matt kept the organization deliberately flat, with 11 to 13 practice and client leads reporting directly to him. Engineers, product managers, and designers worked in balanced teams. Each team had an experienced engineering anchor who kept the leads informed about technical progress, team dynamics, and resource needs. Monday allocation reviews brought Matt, practice leads, and client leads together to assign practitioners across engagements, training, and accelerator development.

He built a hiring engine across internal bench searches, campus recruiting, contract-to-hire, and job requisitions. Engineers completed hands-on TDD pairing auditions; product managers and designers worked through live client briefs. Interviewers recorded independent votes, and Matt held the closing interview and made every final hiring decision. During scaling pushes, this produced 4 to 6 net-new practitioners per week, with interview participation capped to protect delivery time.

New joiners received tailored enablement, then rotated through pairing with experienced practitioners. Development followed an “I do, we do, you do” model, supported by mentors, peer-led tech talks, retrospectives, and career conversations. Because managers did not sit on daily teams, performance feedback came from recent pairing partners, product managers, and anchors, rather than a supervisor’s impressions.

The organization reached 150+ practitioners with near-zero attrition in the first two years. Across CIC delivery, 10-person balanced teams delivered equivalent output to traditional 20-person teams, with 4X faster velocity and zero-defect deployments.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Tell me about his payments work

### A

🐾 Matt’s payments work includes building the payment engine behind JP Morgan Chase ACCESS and reducing integration barriers for American Express’s Virtual Payments Platform.

At JP Morgan Chase, regional payment systems had different data models, entitlement structures, message formats, and regulatory requirements. The WebSeries platform provided a foundation, but the bank’s 135,000+ clients across 180+ countries required capabilities beyond its out-of-box offering.

As Technology Delivery Lead, Matt managed a 40+ person blended team across the US, Mumbai, and Bangalore. He designed and delivered bespoke ACH, wire, FX/cross-border, and tax payment modules. Rather than treating every payment alike, the implementation included payment-type-specific formatting, validation, compliance, and permissions, such as allowing a user to approve wires without authorizing FX initiation. He also implemented Message Authentication Code security so corporate users could verify payment integrity before release.

Matt designed the centralized gateway for message transformation, protocol translation, and routing across 12 countries, replacing fragmented point-to-point integrations. He led payments data migration while preserving client configurations, transaction histories, and regulatory traceability, with no disruption to live payment operations. ACCESS launched in April 2011 as a unified global corporate banking portal. Matt also owned production incident response and cross-team root-cause analysis.

At American Express, the challenge was adoption: the new Virtual Payments Platform had flexible APIs, but clients faced missing SDKs, fragmented documentation, no sandbox, and delayed testing.

Matt co-created a developer portal with Java, .NET, and Python SDKs, sample integrations, use case-driven documentation, and a secure sandbox with refreshed client data. Environment virtualization let developers test earlier without waiting on downstream dependencies. His team used Lean Product and XP practices and validated the experience through a Client Onboarding Test Drive.

API consumption and trial usage increased within 3 months of relaunch. Onboarding time fell, and the portal team delivered over 261 additional story points beyond its delivery commitment.
