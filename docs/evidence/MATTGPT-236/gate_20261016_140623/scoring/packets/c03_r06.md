## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt leads.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At the Accenture Cloud Innovation Center, a rigid hierarchical culture made people slow to share ideas. Fear of blame discouraged experimentation, while teams entering innovation engagements often expected polished requirements and predictable outcomes.

Matt worked to remove those barriers rather than add more process. He made intent explicit, pushed decisions to the people closest to the work, and used transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. He coached teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks.

His “permit to fail” culture had specific practices behind it: non-punitive post-mortems, low-stakes experiments, and structured hypotheses. Teams applied lessons from mistakes to subsequent sprints instead of pointing fingers. Weekly brainstorming sessions considered ideas without judgment, and Matt recognized contributions that advanced shared goals rather than emphasizing individual accolades.

For early-stage products, he set expectations that prototypes and user tests would likely fail, intentionally. He used user research, Jobs to Be Done framing, and rapid testing to challenge assumptions before committing to costly builds. Failed experiments became decision inputs that shaped product direction week by week.

He also brought product managers, designers, and engineers together in balanced teams. They delivered thin-slice, production-ready solutions for rapid user feedback, supported by automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first engineering practices.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams accelerated delivery 4x after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. In the “permit to fail” work, teams experimented more freely, repeat errors declined, and delivery timelines shortened.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Yes. In one engagement, Matt’s delivery leadership left critical safeguards unverified, and a team member bore a consequence.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization for a financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams executing test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production environments. IAM controls, environment segregation, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from earlier phases.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup, but he relied on inherited documentation rather than confirming that the controls were correct. He focused on delivery pressures instead of validating access patterns. He did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs, implement email interception or data masking, or verify that production customer names and email addresses had been scrubbed from downstream environments.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients. This was not an intentional, low-stakes experiment of the kind Matt encouraged in innovation work.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. But the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

Afterward, Matt made IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership “day-one leadership responsibilities” in his delivery approach. His stated lesson was that “assumptions are risks in disguise,” and that leaders own the environment their teams operate in.
