## Turn 1. Visitor: Tell me about a time he failed.

### A

🐾 Here’s a time Matt’s assumptions led to a production-data incident.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization for a financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams running test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production. IAM controls, environment segregation, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from earlier phases, along with documentation that Matt relied on without confirming its accuracy.

His responsibility was to ensure a safe environment setup. Instead, he assumed the IAM configuration and environment boundaries were correct. He focused on delivery pressures rather than validating access patterns, did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes, and did not implement compensating controls such as data masking or email interception. He also did not verify whether production customer names and email addresses had been scrubbed from downstream environments.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. They identified insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed, then worked collectively to implement corrective controls. The client nevertheless demanded that the tester be removed from the project: a systemic failure resulted in an individual consequence.

Afterward, Matt made IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership day-one leadership responsibilities in his delivery approach. His description of the failure is direct: these were assumptions he should have verified rather than inherited.
