## Turn 1. Visitor: How did Matt calculate ROI?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt approached it.

Matt used cost-to-fix versus benefit comparisons, delivery measures, and engagement financials. His stories do not specify a single ROI formula or a calculated ROI percentage.

At RBC, leadership needed to decide whether Bottomline WebSeries could support its corporate banking platform before committing further investment. Existing systems had performance and scalability limitations, and several strategic initiatives were competing for resources. Matt conducted code reviews, stakeholder interviews, and a structured architecture assessment across Data, Application, Technology, and Business Architecture. He created heat maps of the gaps and worked with the enterprise architecture team on target-system models with a transparent “cost-to-fix vs. benefit comparison.” The assessment found that WebSeries lacked the required modularity. RBC avoided that investment path and redirected technical resources to higher-priority initiatives. The story does not give a dollar value for the avoided investment.

For the Cloud Innovation Centers, Matt addressed concerns about pair programming costs by comparing the investment with velocity gains and quality improvements. The reported results were 4X faster velocity, 10-person balanced teams delivering equivalent output to traditional 20-person teams, and zero-defect deployments through TDD and automated pipelines. He also owned the centers’ P&L, budget, and cost management. Those were investment-case measures, not a reported financial ROI percentage.

At Fiserv, the financial accounting was explicit: an \$8.5M program finished 3% under budget, preserving \$255K in margin, avoiding \$500K in contractual penalties, and securing a \$3M extension. Matt managed actuals, forecast variance, contingency release, staffing costs, and scope changes. The story reports those outcomes separately rather than combining them into an ROI figure.

### B

🐾 Here’s how Matt made the investment case.

At the Cloud Innovation Centers, clients questioned why they were paying for two engineers at one keyboard. Traditional delivery teams saw pair programming as inefficient and test-driven development as overhead. Meanwhile, developers were discovering defects late, spending weeks debugging and fixing, and accumulating technical debt.

Matt compared delivery results against traditional teams doing equivalent work. He tracked defect rates, velocity, debugging time versus build time, and knowledge distribution across teams. The comparison was measured after the initial learning curve, rather than treating the cost of learning the practices as the steady-state result. Time recovered from reduced rework and debugging far outweighed time spent writing tests and scenarios.

The documented results included 4X velocity compared with client internal teams doing equivalent work. Across the CIC practice, 10-person balanced teams delivered equivalent output to traditional 20-person teams, with zero-defect deployments through test-driven development and automated pipelines. For engineers, code review happened continuously during pairing, knowledge stopped depending on a single person, and new team members got up to speed faster.

For technology investment decisions, Matt also used a direct cost-to-fix versus benefit comparison. At RBC, he assessed WebSeries against the bank’s requirements through code reviews, interviews, architectural heat maps, and target-system models. The assessment found that the platform lacked the required modularity, preventing further investment in that path and allowing technical resources to move to higher-priority initiatives.

The stories document these comparisons and measured results, but they do not specify a formal ROI formula, a monetized benefit calculation, or a calculated ROI percentage. The 4X figure measures delivery velocity, not financial ROI.
