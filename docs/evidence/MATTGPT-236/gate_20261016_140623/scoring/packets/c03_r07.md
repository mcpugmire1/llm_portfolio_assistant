## Turn 1. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership: empathy, clarity, and purpose.

Teams he inherited were often “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center, role silos and hierarchical structures slowed delivery. Some teams avoided risks because they feared blame; others entered innovation work expecting polished requirements and predictable outcomes when they actually had assumptions to test.

Matt pushed decisions to the people closest to the work. He made intent explicit, used direct feedback and open communication, and coached teams to think clearly rather than just execute tasks. He brought engineers, product managers, and UX architects together in balanced teams with equal participation and shared accountability. Team goals were tied to client and business outcomes, with regular stand-ups, retrospectives, and knowledge-sharing sessions to surface roadblocks and adjust how the team worked.

His “permit to fail” environment included non-punitive post-mortems, transparent sharing of errors, and lean, low-stakes experiments. Instead of pointing fingers, teams applied lessons in subsequent sprints to prevent repeat failures. In product incubation, he set expectations upfront that prototypes and user tests would sometimes fail, then helped teams use that evidence to shape product direction week by week.

That freedom sat alongside engineering practices: pair programming, collaborative coding, automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first development. Client workshops and thin-slice, production-ready deliveries gave teams ways to test ideas and adopt those practices.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. The balanced-team work also reduced handoffs and project delays and achieved zero-defect production code.

## Turn 2. Visitor: Give me an example where that didn’t work.

### A

🐾 Yes. Matt describes a delivery failure where he did not verify the environment his team was working in.

In one financial services platform modernization engagement, Matt was the tech lead across development, QA, UAT, and production environments. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams executing test cycles. IAM controls, environment boundaries, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from prior phases. Matt relied on the existing documentation rather than confirming those controls were correct.

He focused on delivery pressures instead of validating access patterns. He did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes, did not implement compensating controls such as data masking or email interception, and did not verify that production customer names and email addresses had been scrubbed from downstream environments.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. But the non-punitive response he advocated did not extend to the client’s decision: the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. His account calls it “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

Matt now treats IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership as day-one leadership responsibilities. He describes the mistake as relying on “assumptions I should have verified rather than inherited.”

### C

🐾 Yes. At Norfolk Southern, the balanced-team, test-first approach Matt introduced did not initially hold in day-to-day work.

In early 2020, his Cloud Innovation Center team was modernizing the client’s legacy Revenue and Shipment Planning platform. A couple of months in, the client reported rising defects even though dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The credibility hit landed with both the client and Accenture account leadership, just as the pandemic forced the team into remote work.

Matt had put pairing, test-driven development, and continuous integration in place, but the reinforcement and verification were missing. An engineer was building from his own interpretation rather than the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. Coverage looked complete while defects went undetected. The backlog also contained vague stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done.

Matt took ownership in front of the client and account leadership rather than defending the metrics. He ran a bottom-up audit of the codebase and test suite, exposed the gaps, and rebuilt the backlog with testable stories. Frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus worked alongside the engineers to redo code and tests through an “I do, we do, you do” progression.

The follow-through changed too. Pairs rotated daily, test cases became an explicit step before implementation, and standups checked that sequence. New engineers paired with seasoned practitioners. During remote work, pairs traded drivers frequently to reduce fatigue and break mental blocks.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. The practices held after the experts left, and the crisis failure mode did not recur. Matt’s stated lesson was that implementing a practice is only half the work: reinforcement, follow-up, and verification had been missing.
