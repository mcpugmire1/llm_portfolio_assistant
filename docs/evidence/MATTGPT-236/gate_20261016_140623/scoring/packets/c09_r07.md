## Turn 2. Visitor: What’s his leadership style?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership: empathy, clarity, and modern engineering rigor.

Most engineering teams he inherited were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center, the model was flat, but old command-and-control habits kept returning: decisions drifted upward, teams waited for direction, and bottlenecks formed. Matt had to reinforce team autonomy repeatedly, not just announce it.

He put product managers, designers, and engineers together with shared ownership and decision-making authority. Team leads focused on removing obstacles rather than controlling outcomes. He made intent explicit, used direct feedback and open communication, and coached teams to think clearly rather than just execute tasks. Weekly brainstorming sessions considered ideas without judgment, and recognition centered on contributions to shared goals rather than individual accolades.

His coaching also included senior engineers paired with junior colleagues, weekly tech talks, peer-led demos, and learning embedded in live client projects.

At Norfolk Southern, that meant taking ownership when defects rose despite near-complete test coverage. Rather than defending the dashboard, Matt audited the code and tests and exposed the gaps to the client and account leadership. He found tests written after the code and backlog stories without defined scenarios or a definition of done. He rebuilt the backlog, brought in TDD experts to work alongside the team, and used boot camps, coding katas, and an “I do, we do, you do” coaching progression.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Across the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration once they embraced lean product and modern engineering practices.

## Turn 3. Visitor: Give me an example.

### A

🐾 Here’s an example from Norfolk Southern.

In 2020, Matt’s Cloud Innovation Center team was modernizing the railroad’s Revenue and Shipment Planning platform when the client raised concerns about rising defects. The dashboards showed near-complete test coverage. The discrepancy landed in front of both the client and Accenture account leadership, who were already accustomed to waterfall delivery, just as the pandemic forced the team into remote work.

Matt took ownership of the failure rather than defending the metrics. He audited the codebase and test suite and showed the gaps openly. An engineer had been building from his own interpretation instead of the backlog story, then writing tests after the code. The backlog was also part of the problem: stories lacked defined scenarios and a definition of done.

Matt rebuilt the backlog with SMART, testable stories and brought frontend and backend TDD experts from Columbus to work side by side with the team. They redid the code and test cases, then reinforced the practices through boot camps, coding katas, and an “I do, we do, you do” coaching progression. He also restructured ceremonies to keep stakeholders, developers, and product managers working from the same requirements.

After the experts left, Matt paired newer engineers with seasoned practitioners and rotated pairs daily. Test cases became a visible step before implementation, checked at standup rather than assumed from dashboard coverage.

The client stopped finding defects the dashboard could not see. Defect rates dropped, client confidence was restored, and the failure mode did not recur after the outside experts went home.
