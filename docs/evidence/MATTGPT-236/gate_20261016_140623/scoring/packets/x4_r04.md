## Turn 1. Visitor: How did Matt calculate ROI?

### A

🐾 Here’s what Matt measured.

The stories describe cost-to-fix versus benefit comparisons, delivery measures, and financial results, but they do not record a specific ROI formula, payback period, or discounted cash-flow calculation.

At the Cloud Innovation Centers, clients questioned the cost of pair programming, while investment in facilities, training, and engineering practices needed justification. Matt addressed those concerns through measured delivery results: 10-person balanced teams delivering equivalent output to traditional 20-person teams, 4X faster velocity, and zero-defect deployments through test-driven development and automated pipelines. He used client case studies and executive briefings to compare velocity gains and quality improvements against the investment. His team scorecards also tracked lead time, cycle time, throughput, team stability, and backlog health rather than velocity alone.

On the cost side, Matt owned the Atlanta and Tampa centers’ P&L, including chargeability, unbillable downtime, and contractor spend across 150+ practitioners. He required internal bench deployment before approving external contractors. Across delivery engagements, he managed actuals, forecast variance, contingency release, staffing costs, and scope changes.

At RBC, the investment assessment included a transparent “cost-to-fix vs. benefit comparison.” Code reviews, architectural heat maps, and gap/fit analysis identified that WebSeries lacked the modularity the bank required. RBC redirected resources rather than committing further investment. No dollar value for that avoided investment is recorded.

At Fiserv, the recorded financial results were more explicit: an \$8.5M program finished 3% under budget, preserving \$255K, with \$500K in contractual penalties avoided and a \$3M extension secured. Those are separate financial outcomes, not a reported ROI percentage.
