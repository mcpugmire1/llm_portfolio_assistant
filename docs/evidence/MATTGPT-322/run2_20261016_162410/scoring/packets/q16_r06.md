# Question q16, run 6

Labels in this packet: A, B, C, D.

## Visitor: Tell me about a time Matt failed

### A

🐾 Here’s a time Matt’s assumptions led to a real failure.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization delivery for a financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams running test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production environments. IAM controls, environment boundaries, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from earlier phases, along with documentation.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. Instead of verifying those controls, he assumed they were correct. He relied on inherited documentation, did not audit who could trigger downstream email processes, and did not verify that production customer names and email addresses had been masked or scrubbed. Data masking and email interception were not in place as compensating controls.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. They identified insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed from downstream environments, then worked together to implement corrective controls.

The human consequence was direct: the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

Afterward, Matt made IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership day-one leadership responsibilities in his delivery approach, rather than treating inherited configurations as verified facts.

### B

🐾 One failure Matt describes began with assumptions he should have verified.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. They identified insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed, then worked collectively to implement corrective controls.

The consequence also fell on the tester: the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

Afterward, Matt made IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership day-one leadership responsibilities in his delivery approach. His stated lesson was that “assumptions are risks in disguise,” and that leaders own the environment their teams operate in.
