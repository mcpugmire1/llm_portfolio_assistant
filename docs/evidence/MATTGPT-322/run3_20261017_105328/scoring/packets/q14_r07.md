## Visitor: What's Matt's leadership philosophy?

### A

🐾 Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose.

Teams he inherited were often “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” His philosophy starts with trust, clarity, and shared purpose, rather than more process or tools. He makes intent explicit, pushes decisions to the people closest to the work, and coaches teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks.

At the Accenture Cloud Innovation Center, he put engineers, product managers, and UX architects into balanced teams with equal participation and shared accountability. Instead of handing work between separate functions, they used pair programming, collaborative coding, stand-ups, and retrospectives. He connected team goals to client and business outcomes, embedded clients into the teams, and used user research and iteration reviews to decide what to build next.

His “permit to fail” environment addressed teams’ fear of blame. Matt organized non-punitive post-mortems, emphasized lessons rather than finger-pointing, and supported low-stakes experiments with structured hypotheses. In product incubation, he set expectations upfront that prototypes and user tests would likely fail. Teams used those results to challenge assumptions and shape product direction week by week, before committing to costly builds.

He paired that freedom with automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first practices, including TDD and continuous integration. At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. The balanced-team work also produced zero-defect production code, reduced handoffs, and fewer project delays.

### B

🐾 Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose.

The engineering teams he inherited were often “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At the Cloud Innovation Center, his responsibility was to create an environment where engineers, designers, and product teams could do the best work of their careers. That meant addressing fear of blame, unclear decisions, and knowledge gaps, not just changing processes or tools.

Matt makes intent explicit and pushes decisions to the people closest to the work. He uses transparency, direct feedback, and open communication, and coaches teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks. His balanced-team model brings Product, Engineering, and Design together, with clients embedded in the work rather than handing over requirements and waiting for delivery.

His “permit to fail” learning environment includes non-punitive post-mortems, transparent sharing of errors, and lean, low-stakes experiments. In product incubation, he sets expectations that early prototypes and user tests will likely fail. Teams use those results to challenge assumptions and shape product direction week by week, before committing to costly builds.

He pairs that learning environment with engineering practices: automation, continuous delivery, TDD, and continuous integration. For development and mentorship, he paired senior engineers with junior team members, established weekly tech talks and peer-led demos, and embedded learning in live client projects.

Across a 150-person Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration once they embraced lean product and modern engineering practices. Post-mortem learnings prevented repeat failure patterns, while mentorship reduced onboarding time for new hires.
