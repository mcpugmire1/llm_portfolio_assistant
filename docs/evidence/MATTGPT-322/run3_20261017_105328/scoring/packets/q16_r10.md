Labels in this packet: A, B.

## Visitor: Tell me about a time Matt failed

### A

🐾 One example involved an email job in a test environment that reached real customers.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization for a large financial services client. The program had development, QA, UAT, and production environments, with offshore teams executing test cycles under delivery pressure. IAM controls, environment segregation, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from earlier phases.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup, but he relied on inherited documentation instead of verifying it. He assumed the IAM configuration and environment boundaries were correct. He did not audit who could trigger downstream email processes, verify that production customer data had been scrubbed, or implement compensating controls such as data masking and email interception.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. They identified insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed from downstream environments, then worked together to implement corrective controls.

The client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described that as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.” His stated lesson was that leaders own the environment their teams operate in. In his delivery approach afterward, IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership became day-one leadership responsibilities rather than inherited assumptions.

### B

🐾 Here’s a time Matt’s assumptions led to a real incident.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization delivery for a financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams executing test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production environments. IAM controls, environment segregation, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from prior phases, along with documentation.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. Instead of verifying the configuration, he assumed the IAM controls and environment boundaries were correct. He relied on inherited documentation without confirming its accuracy, did not audit who could trigger downstream email processes, and did not verify whether production customer names and email addresses had been masked or scrubbed. Compensating controls, such as data masking and email interception, were not in place.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. They identified insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed from downstream environments. He worked with the team to implement corrective controls rather than treating the tester’s action as the whole problem.

The client nevertheless demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.” His stated lesson was that “assumptions are risks in disguise.” IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership became day-one leadership responsibilities in his delivery approach.
