## Visitor: What's Matt's leadership philosophy?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes the teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” His focus is on removing those barriers: making intent explicit, creating psychological safety so people can speak honestly, and pushing decisions to the people closest to the work.

At Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center, that included establishing a “permit to fail” learning environment. Teams had avoided risks because they feared blame. Matt organized non-punitive post-mortems, emphasized lessons rather than pointing fingers, and supported lean, low-stakes experiments. Teams began experimenting more freely, applied post-mortem learnings to subsequent sprints, and prevented recurrence of identified failure patterns.

He also builds balanced teams: Product + Engineering + Design together. Rather than keeping those roles in separate silos, Matt assembled engineers, product managers, and UX architects with equal participation and shared accountability. He embedded pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and knowledge-sharing sessions. Teams could voice feedback and implement changes autonomously instead of waiting for permission.

His coaching focus is to help teams think clearly, not just execute tasks. He pairs that with automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first practices. At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams accelerated delivery 4x after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. In the balanced-team work, teams reduced handoffs and project delays, improved satisfaction and retention, and achieved zero-defect production code.

### B

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. His goal is to create an environment where engineers, designers, and product teams can do the best work of their careers. Many teams he inherited were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” He focused on removing those barriers rather than adding more process.

He makes intent explicit, pushes decisions to the people closest to the work, and uses transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. He coaches teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks. His non-negotiables include psychological safety, integrity and respect, continuous learning, and “outcomes, not optics.”

At the Accenture Cloud Innovation Center, that included a “permit to fail” culture. Teams had avoided risks because they feared blame. Matt organized non-punitive post-mortems and supported lean, low-stakes experiments with structured hypotheses and controlled iterations. Team members began experimenting more freely, applying lessons to subsequent sprints, and preventing repeat failure patterns. In product incubation, he set expectations that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail: the goal was to reduce risk quickly and cheaply, not be right on the first try.

He also assembled balanced teams of engineers, product managers, and UX architects with equal participation and shared accountability. Pair programming, collaborative coding, and retrospectives replaced siloed work and excessive handoffs. Teams could voice feedback and implement changes autonomously.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams accelerated delivery 4x after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. The balanced-team work also reduced project delays, improved retention, and achieved zero-defect production code.
