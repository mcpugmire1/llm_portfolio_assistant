# Question q16, run 1

Labels in this packet: A, B.

## Visitor: Tell me about a time Matt failed

### A

🐾 Here’s a time Matt’s assumptions led to a real client incident.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization delivery for a Fortune 500 financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams executing test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production environments. IAM controls, environment segregation, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from prior phases, along with existing documentation.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. Instead of verifying the configuration, he assumed the IAM controls and environment boundaries were correct. He relied on inherited documentation, did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes, and did not verify that production customer names and email addresses had been masked or scrubbed. He also had not implemented compensating controls such as data masking or email interception.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. They identified insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed from downstream environments, then worked collectively to implement corrective controls.

The client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described that as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.” Following the incident, he made IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership day-one leadership responsibilities in his delivery approach. His own wording for the lesson was: “assumptions are risks in disguise.”
