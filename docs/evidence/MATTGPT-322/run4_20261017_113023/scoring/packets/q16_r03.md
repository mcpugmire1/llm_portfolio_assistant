# Question q16, run 3

Labels in this packet: A, B.

## Visitor: Tell me about a time Matt failed

### A

🐾 Here’s a time Matt’s assumptions led to a production-data incident.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization delivery for a financial services client. The program had development, QA, UAT, and production environments, with offshore teams executing test cycles under delivery pressure. IAM controls, environment segregation, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from prior phases, along with existing documentation.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. Instead of verifying the configuration, he assumed the IAM controls and environment boundaries were correct. He relied on inherited documentation without confirming its accuracy, did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes, and did not implement compensating controls such as data masking or email interception. He also did not verify whether production customer names and email addresses had been scrubbed from downstream environments.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. The team identified insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed, then worked collectively to implement corrective controls.

The client nevertheless demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.” His stated lesson was that “assumptions are risks in disguise.” IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership became day-one leadership responsibilities in his delivery approach.
