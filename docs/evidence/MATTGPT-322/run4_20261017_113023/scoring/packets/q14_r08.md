## Visitor: What's Matt's leadership philosophy?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. Most engineering teams he inherited were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” His task was to create an environment where engineers, designers, and product teams could do the best work of their careers, with trust, clarity, and shared purpose rather than just process or tools.

He makes intent explicit, pushes decisions to the people closest to the work, and uses transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. He coaches teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks. He also grounds engineering culture in automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first practices, giving teams confidence in what they ship.

At the Accenture Cloud Innovation Center, teams had avoided risks because they feared blame. Matt introduced a “permit to fail” culture through non-punitive post-mortems and lean, low-stakes experiments. Teams discussed lessons rather than pointing fingers, applied those lessons to subsequent sprints, and began treating failed experiments as decision inputs rather than setbacks. Repeat errors declined and project timelines shortened.

He also built balanced teams of engineers, product managers, and UX architects with equal participation and shared accountability. Pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and knowledge-sharing sessions replaced role silos and reduced handoffs. Teams could voice feedback and implement changes autonomously.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, his leadership approach produced 4x delivery acceleration once teams embraced lean product and modern engineering practices. The balanced-team work also reduced project delays, improved team satisfaction and retention, and achieved zero-defect production code across client engagements.

### B

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He builds a “permit to fail” learning environment and brings Product, Engineering, and Design together in balanced teams.

Many teams he inherited were over-processed, under-trusted, and waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes. His focus was to remove those barriers: make intent explicit, create psychological safety so people could speak honestly, and push decisions to the people closest to the work. He coached teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks.

At Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center, that meant assembling engineers, product managers, and UX architects with equal participation and shared accountability. Matt aligned team goals with client and business outcomes, embedded pair programming and collaborative coding, and used stand-ups, retrospectives, and knowledge-sharing sessions to identify roadblocks. Teams could voice feedback and implement changes autonomously rather than wait for approval.

When fear of blame discouraged experimentation, Matt organized non-punitive post-mortems and supported lean, low-stakes experiments. He emphasized lessons learned rather than pointing fingers. In product incubation, he set expectations upfront that prototypes and user tests would sometimes fail, then helped teams use those results to shape product direction week by week.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration once they embraced lean product and modern engineering practices. Balanced teams also achieved zero-defect production code through iterative development and real-time feedback loops.
