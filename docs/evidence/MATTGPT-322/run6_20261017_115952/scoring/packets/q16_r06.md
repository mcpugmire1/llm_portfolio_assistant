Labels in this packet: A, B.

## Visitor: Tell me about a time Matt failed

### A

🐾 Matt’s account of a failure centers on assumptions he did not verify.

He was the tech lead on a global platform modernization delivery for a financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams executing test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production environments. Identity and access management (IAM) controls, environment segregation, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from prior phases, along with existing documentation.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. Instead, he relied on that documentation without confirming its accuracy. He did not validate access patterns, audit who could trigger downstream email processes, or verify whether production customer names and email addresses had been masked or scrubbed. Data masking and email interception were not in place as compensating controls.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. He worked collectively with the team to understand what happened and implement corrective controls. The root causes were insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed from downstream environments.

The human consequence was explicit: the client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence,” and said leaders own the environment their teams operate in.

His stated change afterward was to make IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership day-one leadership responsibilities, rather than treating inherited configurations as verified facts.
