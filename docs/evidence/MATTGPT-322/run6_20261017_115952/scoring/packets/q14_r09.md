## Visitor: What's Matt's leadership philosophy?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes engineering teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At Accenture, his focus was on removing those barriers, not adding more process: making intent explicit, building psychological safety, and pushing decisions to the people closest to the work.

He uses transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. He coaches teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks, and grounds engineering culture in automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first practices. At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams accelerated delivery 4x once they embraced lean product and modern engineering practices. In his words, “It didn’t happen overnight, but it stuck.”

His “permit to fail” environment has specific practices behind it. When teams avoided risks because they feared blame, Matt organized non-punitive post-mortems and supported lean, low-stakes experiments with structured hypotheses and controlled iterations. Teams applied lessons to subsequent sprints to prevent recurring failures. In product incubation, he set expectations that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail intentionally, then used user research, JTBD framing, and rapid testing to shape product direction week by week.

He also brings Product, Engineering, and Design together in balanced teams with equal participation and shared accountability. Rather than preserving role silos and handoffs, he embedded pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and autonomous workflow changes. Those teams reduced project delays and achieved zero-defect production code through iterative development and real-time feedback loops.

### B

🐾 Here's how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. Many engineering teams he inherited were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At Accenture, teams also faced role silos and a fear of blame that discouraged experimentation. His focus was to create an environment where engineers, designers, and product teams could speak honestly, challenge assumptions, and take ownership of their work.

Matt makes intent explicit and pushes decisions to the people closest to the work. He uses transparency, direct feedback, and open communication, and coaches teams to think clearly rather than just execute tasks. He builds balanced teams with engineers, product managers, and UX architects, giving them equal participation and shared accountability. Pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and knowledge-sharing sessions put that into daily practice.

His “permit to fail” environment includes non-punitive post-mortems and lean, low-stakes experiments. Instead of pointing fingers, teams examine mistakes and apply the lessons to subsequent sprints. In early-stage product work, Matt sets expectations that prototypes and user tests will sometimes fail intentionally: teams are testing assumptions before committing to costly builds. Failed experiments become decision inputs rather than setbacks.

Matt also names integrity, respect, continuous learning, and “outcomes, not optics” as non-negotiables. At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. His balanced-team work reduced handoffs and project delays, improved team satisfaction and retention, and achieved zero-defect production code. Teams could raise issues without blame and implement changes autonomously.
