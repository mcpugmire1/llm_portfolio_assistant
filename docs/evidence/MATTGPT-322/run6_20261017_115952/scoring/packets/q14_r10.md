## Visitor: What's Matt's leadership philosophy?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes the starting point in many inherited engineering teams as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At Accenture, teams also faced role silos, hierarchical bottlenecks, and fear of blame that discouraged experimentation. His focus was to create an environment where engineers, designers, and product teams could do the best work of their careers.

He made intent explicit and pushed decisions to the people closest to the work. Instead of relying on more process or approval layers, he used transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. He coached teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks, and grounded engineering work in automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first practices.

His “permit to fail” environment included non-punitive post-mortems and lean, low-stakes experiments. Teams analyzed mistakes without pointing fingers and applied the lessons in subsequent sprints. In early-stage product work, he set expectations that prototypes and user tests would sometimes fail. Teams used user research, JTBD framing, and rapid testing to validate assumptions before committing to costly builds.

He also assembled balanced teams of engineers, product managers, and UX architects with equal participation and shared accountability. Pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and regular feedback replaced siloed handoffs. His stated priorities included clarity of purpose, integrity and respect, and “outcomes, not optics.”

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams accelerated delivery 4x after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. The balanced-team work also reduced project delays and achieved zero-defect production code.

### B

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. Many engineering teams he inherited were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” His focus was to remove those barriers: make intent explicit, build psychological safety so people could speak honestly, and push decisions to the people closest to the work.

At Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center, that meant coaching teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks. He used transparency, direct feedback, and open communication, alongside automation, continuous delivery, and quality-first engineering practices. Across work that included leading a 150-person center, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration once they embraced lean product and modern engineering practices.

His “permit to fail” learning environment addressed teams’ fear of blame directly. Matt organized non-punitive post-mortems, emphasized lessons rather than finger-pointing, and supported lean, low-stakes experiments. In product incubation, he set expectations that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail intentionally: teams were testing assumptions before committing to costly builds. They applied those learnings to subsequent sprints, preventing recurrence of identified failure patterns and reducing wasted engineering effort.

He also built balanced teams: Product + Engineering + Design together. Engineers, product managers, and UX architects shared accountability rather than working through hierarchical handoffs. Pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and real-time feedback helped those teams reduce project delays and achieve zero-defect production code across client engagements.

Matt’s stated non-negotiables include integrity and respect, outcomes rather than optics, and continuous learning. He walks away from opportunities where intensity is not balanced with respect for people, regardless of title.
