## Visitor: What's Matt's leadership philosophy?

### A

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. Many engineering teams he inherited were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” At Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center, teams also avoided risks because they feared blame. Hierarchical structures and separate engineering, design, and product roles created bottlenecks and slowed delivery.

Matt focused on changing those working conditions. He made intent explicit, pushed decisions to the people closest to the work, and used transparency, direct feedback, and open communication. He coached teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks. His stated principles include integrity and respect, continuous learning, and “outcomes, not optics.”

His “permit to fail” environment included non-punitive post-mortems, transparent sharing of mistakes, and low-stakes experiments. Instead of pointing fingers, teams applied lessons to subsequent sprints to prevent repeat errors. In early product work, he set expectations that prototypes and user tests would sometimes fail. Teams used those results to change product direction before committing to costly builds.

He also assembled balanced teams of engineers, product managers, and UX architects with equal participation and shared accountability. Pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, automation, and continuous delivery supported their daily work. Team members could voice concerns and implement changes autonomously.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams accelerated delivery 4x after adopting lean product and modern engineering practices. His balanced-team work reduced handoffs and project delays, improved team satisfaction and retention, and achieved zero-defect production code across client engagements.

### B

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. He describes teams he inherited as “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” His focus is on removing those barriers so engineers, designers, and product teams can speak honestly, make decisions, and take ownership of their work.

He makes intent explicit, pushes decisions to the people closest to the work, and uses transparency and direct feedback. He coaches teams to think clearly, not just execute tasks. His non-negotiables include psychological safety, integrity and respect, continuous learning, and “outcomes, not optics.” He also chooses environments where intensity is balanced with respect for people.

At the Accenture Cloud Innovation Center, that included a “permit to fail” learning environment. Teams had avoided risks because they feared blame. Matt introduced non-punitive post-mortems and lean, low-stakes experiments. Instead of pointing fingers, teams applied lessons to subsequent sprints. In innovation work, he set expectations that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail: the goal was to test assumptions before committing to costly builds.

He also built balanced teams with engineers, product managers, and UX architects sharing accountability, rather than working through hierarchical handoffs. Pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and open feedback gave people ways to share knowledge and change how they worked.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams achieved 4x delivery acceleration after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. The balanced-team work also reduced project delays, improved retention, and achieved zero-defect production code.
