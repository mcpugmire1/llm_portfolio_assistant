### B

🐾 Here’s how Matt describes his leadership philosophy.

Matt leads with empathy, clarity, and purpose. Most engineering teams he inherited were “over-processed, under-trusted, waiting for permission instead of owning outcomes.” He focused on removing those barriers: psychological safety so people could speak honestly, decisions pushed to the people closest to the work, and engineering practices that gave teams confidence in what they shipped.

He makes intent explicit, uses transparency and direct feedback, and coaches teams to think clearly rather than just execute tasks. At Accenture’s Cloud Innovation Center, that included a “permit to fail” learning environment. Teams had avoided risks because they feared blame. Matt organized non-punitive post-mortems, emphasized lessons rather than finger-pointing, and supported lean, low-stakes experiments with structured hypotheses and controlled iterations. Teams applied those lessons to subsequent sprints to prevent repeat failures.

He also built balanced teams with engineers, product managers, and UX architects participating equally and sharing accountability for outcomes. Instead of hierarchical handoffs between separate functions, he embedded pair programming, collaborative coding, retrospectives, and real-time feedback into daily work. Teams could voice concerns and implement changes autonomously.

In innovation work, Matt set expectations that early prototypes and user tests would likely fail, intentionally. He used user research, JTBD framing, and rapid testing to validate assumptions before committing to costly builds.

At the Cloud Innovation Center, teams accelerated delivery 4x after embracing lean product and modern engineering practices. His balanced-team work also achieved zero-defect production code through iterative development and real-time feedback loops.
