# Question q16, run 1

Labels in this packet: A, B, C, D.

## Visitor: Tell me about a time Matt failed

### A

🐾 Here’s a time Matt got it wrong.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization delivery for a financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams running test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production. IAM controls, environment segregation, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from prior phases, along with existing documentation.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup, but he relied on that documentation instead of verifying it. He assumed the IAM configuration and environment boundaries were correct. He did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes, confirm that production customer names and email addresses had been scrubbed, or implement compensating controls such as data masking and email interception.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients. The root cause was insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed from downstream environments.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. He worked collectively with the team to understand what happened and implement corrective controls. The client nevertheless demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described it as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence.”

His lesson was that “assumptions are risks in disguise.” IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership became day-one leadership responsibilities in his delivery approach, rather than controls he assumed were already in place.
