# Question q16, run 9

Labels in this packet: A, B, C, D.

## Visitor: Tell me about a time Matt failed

### A

🐾 Here’s a time Matt failed.

Matt was the tech lead on a global platform modernization delivery for a financial services client. The program was under delivery pressure, with offshore teams running test cycles across development, QA, UAT, and production environments. IAM controls, environment segregation, and downstream access configurations had been inherited from prior phases, along with existing documentation.

Matt was responsible for ensuring a safe environment setup. Instead of verifying the IAM configuration and downstream access controls, he assumed they were correct. He relied on the inherited documentation, did not audit who could trigger downstream jobs or email processes, and did not verify whether production customer names and email addresses had been masked or scrubbed. He also had not implemented compensating controls such as data masking or email interception.

An offshore tester accidentally triggered an email job in UAT. It sent communications containing unmasked production customer data to thousands of real clients.

Matt took accountability and led the team through root cause analysis and remediation. He worked collectively with the team to understand what happened and implement corrective controls. The root cause included insufficient IAM checks, unclear environment boundaries, and production data that had not been scrubbed from downstream environments.

The client demanded that the tester be removed from the project. Matt described that as “a systemic failure that resulted in an individual consequence,” and said leaders own the environment their teams operate in.

Afterward, Matt made IAM validation, environment verification, PII scrubbing, and risk ownership day-one leadership responsibilities in his delivery approach. His description of the failure was direct: these were “assumptions he should have verified rather than inherited.”
